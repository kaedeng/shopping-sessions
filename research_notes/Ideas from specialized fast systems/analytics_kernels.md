# In-memory kernels for the stages after the parse: what engines and papers do that we do not

Reading notes. "Measured" means the source reports it; everything about our pipeline is under **Inferences** and is my arithmetic or judgment, not a measurement. Cycle budgets for our stages are derived from `positional.md` (20M set, 8 workers, 8.55 G cycles in all, profile taken before `attr-wide`): place 5% (about 0.43 G cycles, about 20 per parsed row), the cut and what follows 15% (about 1.28 G), click grouping and sorting 7% (about 0.6 G, about 117 per click; the sort alone was 2.7%).

Checked in the source before writing, so that none of it is presented as new: the purchases' session is already a bitmask-and-popcount rank (`starts[slot >> 4]`, `blk_ns`, `count_ones` in `src/positional.rs`); the empty slot is already "largest ingest", so placing is a minimum; `attr-wide` already does the click-driven sweep with a per-campaign end of the last stretch and per-day prefix sums in a 16-lane register for persons with more than 16 clicks, and compare-per-session plus `vpconflictd` on unsorted clicks for the rest; assists are already campaign-major (`[campaign * DAYS + day]`); the scatter already uses staging lines and streaming stores; the cut already compresses in the register.

Headline: I found no kernel in the literature that would by itself halve the stages after the parse. The items not yet used that look worth a measurement, largest first: an 8-byte "hot" click record and L2-sized click ranges for the grouping scatter (question 1), sorting networks for click lists of 17 to 128 and no sort at all below that (question 2), `user_daily` rows from a second boundary mask (question 5), campaign-major credit counters and a check of repeated-cell increments on the Intel target, where store forwarding costs 4 to 5 cycles and Zen 4 hides it (question 6). Together my estimate is 3 to 6% of CPU time, with wide uncertainty.

## 1. Partitioning and scatter: best fan-out, buffer size and store type; hyperthreads and 2 MB pages

### Takeaway
The measured optimum for a buffered, streamed, single-pass scatter is flat from 32 to about 2,048 partitions and rises after 4,096; one cache line of staging per partition with the fill state and the destination kept inside that line is the best layout from about 1,024 partitions up. Our fan-out (about 1,300 lists per worker at 20M with 2^14-number partitions, about 4,000 at 60M) sits at the knee only at the final size. Record width matters measurably (8 to 16 bytes cost 1.31 to 1.69 times), which points at the click grouping, not the event scatter.

### Cited Findings
- Schuhknecht et al., setup: 100M entries of 4-byte key + 4-byte payload, single thread, Xeon E5-2407 2.2 GHz (no hyperthreading, no turbo), L1 32 KB, L2 256 KB, L3 10 MB, 64 L1 dTLB + 512 L2 TLB entries for 4 KB pages, 32 for 2 MB pages; transparent huge pages on by default. Times include the histogram pass. — [Schuhknecht, Khanchandani, Dittrich, VLDB 2015](http://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol8/p934-schuhknecht.pdf)
- Same paper, unbuffered against buffered (memcpy flush): 1,024 partitions 2.41 s to 1.30 s; 4,096: 2.91 to 2.01; 16,384: 3.61 to 3.00. Only at 32 partitions is the unbuffered version faster (0.79 against 0.83 s). Without streaming the best buffer is several lines and performance degrades once all buffers together pass about 2 MB. — [same](http://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol8/p934-schuhknecht.pdf)
- Same paper, adding non-temporal stores (`_mm256_stream_si256`, 4 entries a store, two stores a line): gain grows with fan-out, 1.12x at 256, 1.20x at 1,024, 1.31x at 4,096, 1.36x at 16,384; it is a small loss below 256. "one should use small buffers (less than 8 cache-lines)"; the best size is a single line from 2,048 partitions on (best sizes 5, 7, 6, 6, 4, 2 lines for 32 to 1,024). Misaligned partition starts are handled by offsetting the first fill and patching the few wrong entries afterwards; padding made no measurable difference. — [same](http://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol8/p934-schuhknecht.pdf)
- Same paper, micro row layout, one-line buffers: keeping the fill state and the write destination in the last slot of the buffer line (overwritten by the last entry, restored from a local after the flush) so that an insert touches one line: 1,024 partitions 1.08 to 0.96 s; 4,096: 1.54 to 1.11 s; 16,384: 2.21 to 1.45 s ("1.52x just due to this effect"). Below 1,024 partitions separate small arrays are better because they stay in L1. Final curve (layout-optimised): 0.87, 0.89, 0.88, 0.87, 0.92, 0.96, 0.99, 1.11, 1.31, 1.45 s for 32 to 16,384 partitions, that is 8.7 to 14.5 ns a tuple at 2.2 GHz. — [same](http://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol8/p934-schuhknecht.pdf)
- Same paper, prefetching the next slot of the destination buffer: helps from 4,096 partitions on, costs time below 1,024. — [same](http://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol8/p934-schuhknecht.pdf)
- Same paper, pages: for the unbuffered version 4 KB pages beat 2 MB pages up to 1,024 partitions (a partition is larger than a huge page, and there are more 4 KB TLB slots); "in combination with software-managed buffers and streaming stores, the 4KB pages do never pay off". — [same](http://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol8/p934-schuhknecht.pdf)
- Same paper, entry size: going from 8-byte to 16-byte entries slows the methods by 1.31x (unbuffered, prefetched) to 1.69x (buffered + streamed, prefetched); 10 times the entries scales linearly. — [same](http://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol8/p934-schuhknecht.pdf)
- Polychroniou and Ross: in-cache variants are bound by TLB capacity; when the data is out of cache their optimum is 5 to 6 bits (32 to 64 partitions); the buffered out-of-cache variants move the optimum to 10 to 12 bits non-in-place and 9 to 10 bits in-place. "The optimal fanout is the one with the highest performance per partitioning bit". With a one-line buffer per partition "the partitioning fanout is now bounded by the number of cache lines in the fast core-private cache, rather than the TLB entries"; the last buffer slot holds the output offset; for columns in separate arrays, one buffer line per column. — [Polychroniou and Ross, SIGMOD 2014](http://www.cs.columbia.edu/~orestis/sigmod14I.pdf)
- Same paper on SMT (1,024-way, 10^9 tuples of 64-bit key and payload): "The in-place variant gets a noticeable benefit from SMT compared to the non-in-place." — [same](http://www.cs.columbia.edu/~orestis/sigmod14I.pdf)
- Balkesen et al.: without buffers "the maximum fanout per radix pass is primarily limited by the number of TLB entries"; best totals were 14 radix bits in two passes on Nehalem and 11 bits in one pass on an AMD machine; in the SMT region "both suffer from the sharing of hardware resources", and "cache-efficient algorithms cannot benefit from SMT threads to the same extent since there are not many cache misses"; "SMT can only remedy cache miss latencies if the respective code contains enough cache misses and enough additional work for the second thread". — [Balkesen, Teubner, Alonso, Özsu, ICDE 2013](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~hebs/course/cs6284/papers/Balkesen-icde2013.pdf)
- Wassenberg and Sanders: (a) reserve address space for N items per bucket and let the kernel map pages on first touch, which removes the counting pass; (b) software write-combining: one 64-byte line per bucket in cache, flushed with an unrolled loop of non-temporal stores, because CPUs have only "four to ten write-combine (WC) buffers" and streams beyond that force partial flushes; with 2^8 buckets the buffers, pointers, output positions and counters fit a 32 KB L1. Measured on a dual-CPU 3.2 GHz i7-class machine, 8 cores, 32-bit keys, 4 passes: 334 M keys/s without and 621 M/s with write-combining; with 32-bit values 203 and 430 M/s. They map buckets with small pages and inputs with large pages. — [Wassenberg and Sanders, arXiv 1008.2849](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1008.2849)
- Polychroniou, Raghavan, Ross (2015), vectorised partitioning: histograms replicated W times so that lane j counts in `H[i*W + j]` (no conflicts, reduced at the end); offsets by gather + scatter with a conflict-serialisation loop; buffered shuffling with W slots a partition flushed by non-temporal stores. All of it rests on gathers and scatters; Haswell had no scatter, and most numbers are from Xeon Phi. — [Polychroniou et al., SIGMOD 2015](http://www.cs.columbia.edu/~orestis/sigmod15.pdf)
- Skylake-X (the core Cascade Lake keeps): L1D 32 KB 8-way, L2 1,024 KB 16-way at 14 cycles, data TLB 64 entries for 4 KB pages and 32 for 2 MB, STLB 1,536 entries; an L1 TLB miss that hits the STLB costs 9 cycles. — [7-cpu, Skylake-X](https://www.7-cpu.com/cpu/Skylake_X.html)
- Secondary sources quoting Intel's optimisation manual say that on Skylake the DTLB and the 1,536-entry STLB are statically split between the two threads of a core when hyperthreading is on (the 4 KB ITLB is shared dynamically). — [Intel community thread](https://community.intel.com/t5/Intel-Moderncode-for-Parallel/Hyper-threading-on-Skylake/m-p/1142414); [Stack Overflow mirror](https://kiwix.lema.org/stackoverflow.com_en_all_2023-11/questions/51360825/does-a-hyper-threaded-core-share-mmu-and-tlb)

### Inferences
- **Event scatter.** With hyperthreads each worker has about half of L1 (16 KB, 256 lines) and half of L2 (512 KB, 8,192 lines). One-line staging for 1,300 lists (20M) is 83 KB, for 4,000 lists (60M) 256 KB: L2-resident at both sizes, L1-resident at neither. Schuhknecht's curve says 1,024 to 2,048 partitions cost 10 to 14% more than 256, and 4,096 about 28% more (per tuple, with 8-byte tuples, a whole core). So our reported insensitivity to 2^12 to 2^16 at 20M is what the paper predicts, and at 60M the 2^14 setting is at the knee: 2^15 or 2^16 numbers a partition there would keep the fan-out near 1,000 to 2,000 if the place stage tolerates 512 KB to 1 MB of slots (it will not fit the per-thread L2 share at 2^16). Worth one paired run at 60M only.
- **Fill state inside the staging line** is the one scatter technique I could not confirm we use: if a list's write position and its staging fill are in arrays apart from the staging line, an insert touches 2 to 3 lines. Measured gain in the paper: 1.12x at 1,024 and 1.39x at 4,096 partitions. Our scatter is 7% of cycles, so the ceiling is roughly 1% at 20M and 2% at 60M. Needs nothing beyond SSE2/AVX streaming stores.
- **Click grouping is the scatter the literature would not write the way we do.** It is a counting scatter with one destination per person (fan-out in the thousands with 1 to 8 records each), 16-byte records, into a stretch that for a 2^13-person range is about 1 MB and for the first range (a fifth of the clicks) about 16 MB. Every write is a read-for-ownership miss beyond L2. Two measured facts apply: record width (16 against 8 bytes: 1.31 to 1.69x) and the rule that an unbuffered scatter should stay inside the private cache. Concrete form: (a) scatter an 8-byte hot record `ts << 32 | campaign << 16 | spare` (a person is known from the position, so `u` is dead weight after grouping) and write the 52-bit `click_id` key to a parallel cold array read only when two of a person's clicks share a `ts` and differ in campaign; the sort and `attr-wide` then touch half the bytes, and 16 clicks are two registers of 64-bit lanes (`vpsrlq` / `vpmovqd` give the 32-bit `ts` and campaign lanes); (b) choose the click shift so that a range's stretch is at most about 256 KB (about 2,000 persons with 16-byte records, 4,000 with 8-byte ones), the same reasoning as the 256 KB of slots. Grouping is about 4% of cycles; I would expect 1 to 1.5% of CPU time from (a) and (b) together. AVX-512 F only for the lane extraction.
- **2 MB pages and the slot array.** 256 KB of slots is 64 4-KB pages; if the DTLB really is split 32/32 under hyperthreading, a 4 KB-backed slot array misses the L1 TLB on roughly half its random writes at 9 cycles each (up to 4 to 5 of place's 20 cycles a row). Since a 2^12 partition (16 pages) measured the same as 2^14, either the array is already on a huge page or this is hidden by overlap; a `perf stat -e dtlb_store_misses.stlb_hit` on the Intel box settles it. The buffered + streamed lists should stay on 2 MB pages (the paper: 4 KB never pays there).
- **Wassenberg's virtual-memory trick** is what the 4 KB chunk chains of commit `fc2dc7c` approximate; nothing further to take.
- Vectorised histogram/shuffle (Polychroniou 2015) needs gathers and scatters: ruled out by our own measurements.

### Gaps
- I did not read Intel's manual table itself for the TLB split; two secondary sources agree but one names a different table number.
- No source measured partitioning with two hyperthreads sharing L1/L2 on a Skylake-class core with 16-byte tuples; the halved-cache reasoning above is mine.
- DuckDB, ClickHouse, Velox and Polars partitioning internals (fan-out constants, whether they use write-combining buffers) were not read in this pass.

## 2. Small sorts: 2 to 64 keys, and a few lists of thousands

### Takeaway
For fixed small sizes the fastest published method is a sorting network run on whole registers (minimum and maximum between registers, permutes only in the merge steps); vqsort's base case sorts up to 256 keys as a 16-row matrix in "several hundred CPU cycles". For sizes of 1 to 8 the library entry points are slower than a plain inlined insertion sort because call overhead, cold code and mispredictions dominate. A tiered scheme fits our lists: nothing at or below 16 (the wide attribution does not need order), a network for 17 to 128, a vector quicksort or the current sort above.

### Cited Findings
- vqsort base case: inputs of at most 256 keys are copied to an aligned buffer and viewed as a matrix with 16 rows and up to 16 columns (one column per lane), padded with "neutral elements (the last value in sort order)"; the 16 rows are sorted with Green's 16-input network (60 comparators in the list cited below), each comparator one vector minimum and one maximum between two registers; the sorted columns are then merged with bitonic merge networks using lane permutations, without transposing. A 256-key network runs in "several hundred CPU cycles". — [Blacher et al., "Vectorized and performance-portable Quicksort", arXiv 2205.05982](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.05982)
- vqsort large inputs (single core, Xeon Gold 6154 Skylake-SP, 1M uniform keys): AVX-512 1,161 MB/s for i32, 1,137 for i64, 1,142 for u128; AVX2 795, 628, 569; `std::sort` 58, 118, 128; ips4o 152, 284, 287. 128-bit keys (a 64-bit key with 64 bits of data) are pairs of 64-bit lanes compared with a few extra operations, "about 0.7 times as fast as native 64-bit sorts". Partition uses compress-store, unrolled 4 times; "CompressStore can only execute every other clock cycle on Skylake"; 16-bit lanes need VBMI2. — [same](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.05982)
- Best known network sizes: 8 inputs 19 comparators in 6 layers (optimal), 16 inputs 60 in 10 layers (or 61 in 9), 32 inputs 185 in 14. — [Dobbelaere, list of sorting networks](https://bertdobbelaere.github.io/sorting_networks.html)
- Bramas, AVX-512 on Skylake: bitonic networks in registers for up to 16 vectors (128 doubles or 256 integers), a layer being a permute, a minimum, a maximum and a masked move; arrays of other lengths are padded. "AVX-512-bitonic always delivers better performance than the Intel IPP for any size, and better performance than the STL when sorting more than 5 values. The speedup is significant, and is around 8 in average"; the whole hybrid quicksort is 4x GNU `std::sort` and 1.4x IPP. Key/value variant: the same permutes and masks applied to a second register of values. — [Bramas, arXiv 1704.08579](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.08579)
- Intel x86-simd-sort: AVX-512 and AVX2 quicksort, quickselect, argsort and key-value sort; `-march=skylake-avx512` suffices for 32- and 64-bit keys, 16-bit keys need `icelake-client` (VBMI2); key-value sort and argsort exist for 32- and 64-bit types only. — [intel/x86-simd-sort README](https://github.com/intel/x86-simd-sort/blob/main/src/README.md)
- Independent measurement of both libraries: "vqsort as tested is exceedingly slow for small inputs"; x86-simd-sort is faster than vqsort below about 200 keys on Linux (below 10,000 on Windows); for tiny inputs "the function call overhead, cold code, cache- and branch-prediction misses will limit throughput"; x86-simd-sort has a bad-pivot pathology (a pattern with 5% random values ran 14 to 26 times slower than vqsort at 10M). — [Bergdoll, sort-research-rs, x86-simd-sort analysis](https://github.com/Voultapher/sort-research-rs/blob/main/writeup/intel_avx512/text.md)
- ipnsort (now Rust's `sort_unstable`): a dedicated inlined insertion sort for N <= 20 ("Profiling shows that 95+% of inputs are of len <= 20. In such scenarios, i-cache misses are often the most important factor"), and above that small-sorts built from sorting networks and bidirectional merges that are "~2-3x times faster" than the insertion-sort small-sort they replaced, by avoiding mispredictions. — [Bergdoll, ipnsort write-up](https://github.com/Voultapher/sort-research-rs/blob/main/writeup/ipnsort_introduction/text.md)
- ClickHouse's ASOF structure checks `std::is_sorted` before sorting ("The right side is often already sorted by the ASOF key"), uses LSD radix sort only for large arrays ("For small arrays comparison sort is faster"). — [ClickHouse `RowRefs.cpp`](https://github.com/ClickHouse/ClickHouse/blob/master/src/Interpreters/RowRefs.cpp)

### Inferences
- **Lists of at most 16 clicks need no sort** on the fast lane (`attr-wide` reads them unsorted). If stage 4 still sorts every person's list before attribution, that work is dead for about 95% of the lists; sort lazily, only on the path that needs order. This is the cheapest item here and may already be done.
- **17 to 128 clicks: a 16-row network on 64-bit lanes.** Key per lane `ts << 32 | index in the person's list` (or the 8-byte hot record of question 1 directly, with the campaign in the low bits, which makes the sorted register the final data and removes the permutation by index). 16 rows of 8 lanes hold 128 keys: 60 comparators of `vpminuq` + `vpmaxuq` between registers (120 instructions, no permutes), then the column merges. AVX-512 F only (`vpminuq`/`vpmaxuq`, `vpermq`, mask blends): Cascade Lake suffices. Pad with all-ones. Equal `ts` with different campaigns (the generator makes such pairs on purpose) is found afterwards by one compare of neighbours and fixed from the cold key array; equal `ts` with the same campaign needs nothing.
- **Above 128:** vqsort-style partition (compare with the pivot, compress in the register, store whole) down to the network, or keep the current sort; these lists are few (1,290 persons above 256 clicks) and we measured that a radix sort changes nothing.
- **What it could save.** A comparison sort of about 12 random keys costs on the order of 40 compares and a handful of mispredictions at 16.5 cycles each ([7-cpu](https://www.7-cpu.com/cpu/Skylake_X.html)), several hundred cycles; a network is tens. The sort was 2.7% of cycles before `attr-wide`; the ceiling is that 2.7%, a realistic figure 1 to 2% of CPU time, most of it from not sorting short lists at all.
- An is-sorted check before sorting (one pass of neighbour compares) is worth it only if a person's clicks often arrive in time order; `group_clicks` keeps file order, so that depends on the generator's click order, which I did not check.

### Gaps
- No source gives cycles per key for networks at sizes 2 to 16 on Skylake-X; "several hundred cycles for 256 keys" and Bramas's "8x over STL" are the only numbers found.
- I found no published AVX-512 "rank sort" (rank of each key by compare-to-mask and popcount, then a permute): searches returned only networks and quicksorts. It would be straightforward for at most 8 or 16 keys but I cannot cite a cost.
- Instruction latencies of `vpminuq`/`vpmaxuq` and the register form of `vpcompressd` on Cascade Lake were not looked up (uops.info not fetched).

## 3. As-of and interval joins

### Takeaway
Every engine read implements "latest row at or before t" as sort per key plus either a merge or a binary search, with a brute-force loop for small inputs; none has a vector kernel for tiny lists. Our compare-per-session in a register is already the vector form (rank by compare and mask), so there is little to import beyond two small variants.

### Cited Findings
- DuckDB: the right side is hash-partitioned on the equality keys and sorted on the inequality key; probe rows are partitioned and sorted the same way; matching partitions are merge-joined, stopping at the first match. 5M-row build, 50 keys: 0.425 s against 3.5 s for the general inequality join. — [DuckDB, "AsOf Joins: Fuzzy Temporal Lookups"](https://duckdb.org/2023/09/15/asof-joins-fuzzy-temporal-lookups.html)
- DuckDB later added a nested-loop plan for a small left side: `asof_loop_join_threshold`, default 64 rows, "a quadratic operation". — [DuckDB, "Planning AsOf Joins"](https://duckdb.org/2025/02/19/asof-plans.html)
- ClickHouse (hash-join path): each equality key owns a `SortedLookupVector` of (asof value, row reference); it is sorted once, lazily, by the first thread that probes (already-sorted check, LSD radix sort for integer keys when large, stable comparison sort otherwise); a probe is an "Unrolled version of upper_bound and lower_bound", a branch-free binary search. — [ClickHouse `RowRefs.cpp`](https://github.com/ClickHouse/ClickHouse/blob/master/src/Interpreters/RowRefs.cpp)
- QuestDB: default "Fast" algorithm is a binary search on the right table's timestamp (for small search spaces a short linear search first), then a backward scan for the join key; `asof_memoized` remembers per symbol where the last backward scan started and ended; `asof_dense` switches to a forward scan collecting keys in a hash table; `asof_linear` is a forward-only scan. A `TOLERANCE` lets the backward scan stop early. — [QuestDB optimiser hints](https://questdb.com/docs/concept/sql-optimizer-hints/); [QuestDB ASOF JOIN](https://questdb.com/docs/reference/sql/asof-join/)
- kdb+ `aj`: fast only with `g#` on the first column and time sorted within it in memory (`p#` on disk); "aj should run at a million or two trade records per second". — [kx reference, `aj`](https://code.kx.com/q/ref/aj/)
- The vector form of a small lower bound in the database literature is Polychroniou and Ross's range-partition index: load k registers of sorted delimiters, compare all with the key, and take the count of true lanes as the rank; nodes of fan-out `k*W + 1`; 4.95 to 5.8x over scalar binary search for 32-bit keys. — [Polychroniou and Ross, SIGMOD 2014](http://www.cs.columbia.edu/~orestis/sigmod14I.pdf)

### Inferences
- kdb's "one or two million rows a second" and DuckDB's 5M rows in 0.4 s are far slower per row than our kernel; these engines are not a source of a faster inner loop, only of structure (sort lazily, check sortedness, brute force when small).
- **Variant worth a look for at most 16 clicks:** if the clicks sit in the register in `(ts, tie)` order, the credit is the highest set bit of the in-window mask (`lzcnt` on the mask) instead of a masked horizontal maximum and a second compare to find its lane. That trades one 60-comparator network per person (about 5 sessions each) for several instructions per session; I expect a wash for the typical person and a small gain for persons with many sessions. AVX-512 F only.
- For more than 16 clicks our stretch-per-click sweep is the merge every engine uses; galloping would only help when one side is far longer than the other, which the per-click advance of both ends already handles.

### Gaps
- kdb's internals (that `aj` is a binary search, `bin`, inside the group) are not stated on the reference page; I could not confirm them from a primary source.
- ClickHouse's `full_sorting_merge` ASOF path and Velox/Polars as-of joins were not read.

## 4. Distinct keys in a sliding window

### Takeaway
The general-purpose answer in engines (a merge sort tree over "previous occurrence" positions, logarithmic per frame) is much heavier than what our case allows. With results needed only as counts per (campaign, day), the cheapest known formulation is the one `attr-wide` already uses; the only alternative of interest is a stateless variant on clicks ordered by (campaign, ts).

### Cited Findings
- DuckDB evaluates windowed `DISTINCT` aggregates with merge sort trees, vectorised like its segment trees (leaf values and tree states accumulated and flushed 2,048 rows at a time, which gave "about a factor of four" for segment trees), and cites Vogelsgesang et al. — [DuckDB, "Flying Through Windows"](https://duckdb.org/2025/02/14/window-flying.html); paper: [Vogelsgesang et al., SIGMOD 2022](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3514221.3526184)
- DuckDB's own caution, as summarised by the search result (page not read in full): windowed distinct counts work but can be very slow. — [DuckDB, "Catching up with Windowing"](https://duckdb.org/2025/02/10/window-catchup.html)

### Inferences
- **Stateless variant.** Sort a person's clicks by `campaign << 32 | ts`. A campaign's clicks are then adjacent, the union of their stretches is built in a register (start of the first, extend while the next click's stretch begins before the current end), and the campaign's 16 day counts are added to its counter row once per campaign instead of once per click; the per-campaign "end of last stretch" table and whatever resets it between persons disappear. The cost is that a click's first session is no longer monotone across the whole list (it restarts per campaign), so it is a rank against the session starts: one compare and a popcount when the person has at most 16 sessions, a restarted two-pointer otherwise; and the credit still needs time order, so this would be a second ordering of the same keys. I would only try it if the per-campaign state shows up in a profile.
- A bitset form (a 64-bit mask of sessions per click, OR per campaign, popcount against per-day masks) works for the 94% of persons with at most 64 events but replaces one prefix-sum difference by 16 popcounts; not better.
- Merge sort trees, Fenwick trees over the query index and difference arrays all pay a logarithm or a second pass that our sorted, per-person, 16-day shape does not need.

### Gaps
- I could not read the SIGMOD 2022 paper itself (publisher page); that it counts distinct values as "rows in the frame whose previous occurrence lies before the frame start" is my recollection, not verified in this pass.
- No source was found for the offline Fenwick-tree method (queries sorted by right end, last occurrence kept) beyond competitive-programming folklore, so it is not cited.

## 5. Sessionization and run detection

### Takeaway
Nothing in the sources goes beyond "compare with the previous element, turn the result into a mask, compress or count". The additions that exist are about the surroundings: prefix sums in a register when run numbers are needed, working on masks instead of compressed vectors, and not compressing when the block is dense.

### Cited Findings
- In-register prefix sum for 16 lanes of 32 bits: four steps of "shift by 1, 2, 4, 8 lanes and add", the shift done with `valignd` against zero (AVX-512 has no whole-register byte shift); the block's total is carried into the next 16 elements. Single thread on a Xeon Platinum 8175M (Skylake): "nearly three billion floats per second", the scalar loop 3.5 times slower; the "vertical" and tree forms are about 2 times slower than this horizontal one. — [Zhang, Wang, Ross, "Parallel Prefix Sum with SIMD", ADMS 2020](http://www.adms-conf.org/2020-camera-ready/ADMS20_05.pdf)
- Selection in vectorised engines: with AVX-512 a compare gives a mask and a compress gives the selection vector; gains of up to 8.4x in a dense micro-benchmark, falling to parity once the input is sparse (below 50% selected) or out of cache. — [Kersten et al., VLDB 2018](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p2209-kersten.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Dense fast path in the cut.** A 16-slot block whose keep mask is all ones (my guess: 85 to 90% of blocks, since only invalid kept copies leave holes) needs neither `vpcompressd` (two per block today, on the one shuffle port) nor the lane count; the "previous event" can be an unaligned reload one slot back instead of a carried lane. Saves a few shuffle-port instructions per 16 events: under 0.5% of CPU time, but it is the port the sibling thread also needs.
- **`user_daily` rows by a second boundary mask.** `Day::flush` is 90 instructions a row according to the "Not done yet" list of `positional.md` (possibly stale: the counts are already differences of ordinals after the stages were rebuilt, so check what is left of it first). The rows are runs of equal (person, day) over the session records, and their `sessions` and `events` are differences of ordinals at the run starts, so the same kernel applies one level up: compare (user, day) with the previous session, 16 a step, compress the ordinals and ids at the boundaries, subtract neighbours, store the columns whole. Revenue and category stay scalar for the 3% of events that are purchases. If there are a few million rows this is 1 to 1.5% of CPU time. AVX-512 F (and VL for 256-bit forms).
- A session's day for 16 sessions at once is a multiply-high by the reciprocal of 86,400,000 (`vpmuludq`, twice for even and odd lanes) or, per session, one compare of the broadcast `ts` against the 16 day boundaries and a popcount of the mask; either removes a division or a table walk if one is there now.
- Run numbers by prefix sum are not needed: we already keep the session-start bitmask and rank into it by popcount.

### Gaps
- I did not find engine code for sessionization as such (ClickHouse's `sequenceMatch`/`windowFunnel`, Vertica's `CONDITIONAL_TRUE_EVENT`, Polars `rle_id`) and did not read Arrow's run-end kernels; the statement that nothing goes beyond compare-and-compress rests on the papers above, not on a survey of those.
- The share of all-kept blocks and the number of `user_daily` rows are guesses to be replaced by counts.

## 6. Aggregation into small dense tables from several threads

### Takeaway
Per-thread dense tables merged at the end are what every source does when the group count is small; we have that. The two measured effects we may not have accounted for: repeated increments of one cell serialise through store forwarding (4 to 5 cycles on Skylake-class cores, close to zero on Zen 4, so our development machine hides it), and a cell's layout decides how many lines an update touches.

### Cited Findings
- Store forwarding on Skylake: "A memory write followed by a read from the same address takes 4 clock cycles in the best case for operands of 32 or 64 bits, and 5 clock cycles for other operand sizes." Zen 4 "can mirror memory operands inside the CPU so that memory operands have no latency at all in some cases"; Ice Lake and Tiger Lake show a zero-latency forwarding in certain cases. — [Agner Fog, "The microarchitecture of Intel, AMD, and VIA CPUs"](https://www.agner.org/optimize/microarchitecture.pdf)
- Byte histogram, the four-table trick: `count1[*p++]++; count2[*p++]++; ...`, summed at the end, so equal neighbours hit different cells. Core i5-3340M: naive 1,560 MB/s on random data but 580 MB/s when one value is 90% of the input; four tables 1,780 and 1,700 MB/s. The author's explanation after discussion is store-to-load forwarding; a commenter (Kurz) attributes it to speculative loads and memory disambiguation. — [Collet, "Counting bytes fast"](https://fastcompression.blogspot.com/2014/09/counting-bytes-fast-little-trick-from.html)
- TurboHist, i7-9700K (a Skylake-family core), 1 GB inputs: uniform text: naive 1.34 cycles a byte, 8 tables with 64-bit reads 0.90; skewed (BWT) input: naive 3.06, four tables 1.33, 8 tables with 64-bit reads 0.93, the same with run detection 0.72; all zeros: naive 4.08. "Portable scalar functions faster than SIMD functions". — [powturbo/Turbo-Histogram](https://github.com/powturbo/Turbo-Histogram)
- Vector histograms avoid conflicts by one table per lane (`H[i*W + j]`), or detect them (`vpconflictd`, not available on the CPUs of the paper), with gathers and scatters. — [Polychroniou et al., SIGMOD 2015](http://www.cs.columbia.edu/~orestis/sigmod15.pdf)
- Engines: Velox uses a flat array indexed by a value id computed from the key ranges ("kArray", no hashing, capped at 2M entries); the test engines of Kersten et al. run "a pre-aggregation [that] handles heavy hitters and spills groups into partitions" per thread; HyPer's morsel-driven workers take about 100,000 tuples at a time. — [Velox hash table docs](https://github.com/facebookincubator/velox/blob/main/velox/docs/develop/hash-table.rst); [Kersten et al., VLDB 2018](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p2209-kersten.pdf); [Leis et al., SIGMOD 2014](https://db.in.tum.de/~leis/papers/morsels.pdf)
- A partition buffer's layout alone (which fields share the line that is touched) was worth up to 1.52x in the partitioning study. — [Schuhknecht et al., VLDB 2015](http://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol8/p934-schuhknecht.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Measure the counting loops on the Intel target, not on Zen 4.** The loop that counts the stored assist cells of the at-most-16-click path, the credit increments (40% of sessions hit the `organic` row, 30% of clicks three to five campaigns) and the 64-byte load-add-store of a campaign's 16 day counters all repeat cells back to back. On Cascade Lake each repeat is a 5 to 6 cycle chain (and a 512-bit reload of a line just stored is the "other operand sizes" case); on Zen 4 it can be free. Remedies in the sources' order of cost: accumulate a run in a register and add once (we do this per stretch already; do it for the credit of consecutive sessions of one person and day too), or two interleaved tables for the scalar counting loop. Expected gain under 0.5%, but it is an Intel-only cost our box cannot show.
- **Credit counters campaign-major.** They are day-major (`agg_dense.rs`), so a person's sessions credited to one campaign on three days touch three lines 8 KB apart; campaign-major, 16 days of 32-bit counts are one line, as the assists already are. All per-worker counters together are about 560 KB (4.5 MB over 8 workers), more than a hyperthread's share of L2, so the number of lines touched is what counts. Small gain, cheap change.
- Counter width: 32-bit lanes for assists are right for the 16-lane add; 16-bit lanes would halve the table but can overflow per worker and need `vpaddw` on 32 lanes (AVX-512 BW, available) plus a flush rule; not worth it.
- Shared tables with conflict detection have no case here: 28K cells a worker is cheap to keep per thread and to sum.

### Gaps
- `vpconflictd`'s cost on Cascade Lake against Zen 4 was not looked up; I recall it being tens of micro-operations on Skylake-X, which would again be hidden on our box, but at once per person it is at most about 0.1% of CPU time either way.
- I found no measurement of repeated-key increments with 512-bit read-modify-write of a whole counter row.

## 7. Deduplication with dense keys

### Takeaway
With a dense key the literature's answer is direct addressing, which is what we do; the remaining choices (clear each time, generation tags, or a bitmap beside the data) are not compared anywhere I read, and my arithmetic says the clear is too cheap to matter.

### Cited Findings
- Velox switches a hash table to a directly indexed flat array whenever the combined key range is small (up to 2M entries): "Lookup is a single array access — no hashing, no tag comparison, no probing." — [Velox hash table docs](https://github.com/facebookincubator/velox/blob/main/velox/docs/develop/hash-table.rst)
- In-cache scatter to more destinations than the TLB holds is TLB-bound (Polychroniou and Ross); an L1 TLB miss with an STLB hit costs 9 cycles on Skylake-X. — [Polychroniou and Ross, SIGMOD 2014](http://www.cs.columbia.edu/~orestis/sigmod14I.pdf); [7-cpu](https://www.7-cpu.com/cpu/Skylake_X.html)

### Inferences
- Clearing 256 KB between partitions is 8,192 256-bit stores for up to 16,384 rows, about half a store a row against place's 20 cycles a row: a generation tag (the competing entry's choice) would save well under 1% and adds a compare to every row. Not worth copying.
- A presence bitmap (2 KB for 16,384 slots) instead of the "largest ingest" sentinel would let the cut read 16 keep bits from the bitmap rather than derive them from four loads of slots, but the cut needs the slots anyway.
- Place is a load-compare-store per row with independent addresses; if the "is the stored ingest smaller" test is a branch, 7.4% duplicates mispredict at 16.5 cycles, about 1.2 cycles a row averaged; a conditional move on both words removes it. I did not read `place_rows` closely enough to know which it is.

### Gaps
- No paper found on never-cleared, generation-tagged tables against cleared ones; Briggs and Torczon's sparse set (which `attr-sweep` already uses) is the classic reference but I did not re-read it in this pass, so it is not cited.

## 8. Design principles: fused pipelines against separate tight passes

### Takeaway
The one controlled comparison (same machine class as our target, Skylake-X with AVX-512) says: fuse when the work is computation on data that is in cache, split into simple per-column loops when the work waits on memory; SIMD gains are large only in cache; and with both hyperthreads busy the difference between the two styles shrinks, because the second thread fills in the gaps that less efficient code leaves.

### Cited Findings
- Kersten et al., i9-7900X (10 cores, 2 hyperthreads each, AVX-512, 1 MB L2), TPC-H SF 100, one thread: the fused engine ("Typer") against the vectorised one ("Tectorwise"): Q1 (computation, cache-resident aggregation) 4,426 against 7,871 ms; Q6 1,511 against 1,443; Q3 9,754 against 7,627; Q9 28,086 against 20,371; Q18 13,620 against 18,072. The vectorised engine "executes significantly more instructions (up to 2.4x) and usually has more L1 data cache misses (up to 3.3x)" because it "must materialize intermediate results", yet wins the join queries because its simple loops let the out-of-order core keep more misses in flight. — [Kersten et al., VLDB 2018](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p2209-kersten.pdf)
- Same paper, hyperthreads: from 10 threads (one per core) to 20, speed-up over one thread goes 8.9 to 9.5 (fused) and 9.1 to 11.1 (vectorised) on Q1; 8.7 to 11.6 and 8.4 to 10.3 on Q3; 6.5 to 7.0 and 7.4 to 8.9 on Q18. "For all but one query, the performance gap between the two systems becomes smaller when all 20 hyper-threads are used"; "hyper-threading is effective at hiding some of the downsides of microarchitecturally sub-optimal code". — [same](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p2209-kersten.pdf)
- Same paper, SIMD: up to 8.4x in micro-benchmarks on L1-resident data; hashing 2.3x, gathers 1.1x ("at most two load operations per cycle—regardless of whether SIMD gather or scalar loads are used"), a whole probe primitive 1.4x, whole join queries about 10%: "SIMD is only beneficial when all data fits into the cache." Vector size: about 1,000 values is best, below 64 or above 64K clearly worse. — [same](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p2209-kersten.pdf)
- Relaxed operator fusion: break a fused pipeline at chosen points into stages that pass small vectors, so that the stage after can prefetch and use SIMD on full vectors; up to 2.2x over full fusion on data larger than the caches. — [Menon, Mowry, Pavlo, VLDB 2017](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p1-menon.pdf)
- Agner Fog on two threads in a core (in the chapter on a recent Intel core that follows the Skylake one; I did not note which): "Most of the critical resources are shared between two threads running in the same core, including instruction fetching, decoders, µop cache, and level-1 caches"; and (in the Knights Landing chapter) running several threads per core pays only "when the performance is limited by memory access, branch mispredictions, or long dependency chains". — [Agner Fog, microarchitecture manual](https://www.agner.org/optimize/microarchitecture.pdf)
- Balkesen et al.: cache-efficient code gains little from the second thread; code with many misses gains more. — [Balkesen et al., ICDE 2013](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~hebs/course/cs6284/papers/Balkesen-icde2013.pdf)

### Inferences
- These results match what `positional.md` already measured: the fused decode, which keeps the vector units busy, took twice as long with a sibling running, while the tokenizer path took 1.6 times. For a score that is CPU time on hyperthreads, the criterion for a kernel is therefore the cycles it occupies the shared execution ports and L1 for, not its instruction count: a loop that waits on L2 or on mispredictions costs the pair of threads less than its own cycle count suggests, and a dense vector loop costs more.
- Place-then-cut in one task while the slots are in L2 is the fused form the paper favours for cache-resident computation, and "passes over records of one kind" afterwards is the vectorised form; the literature gives no reason to change that split. The Q1 result (fused 1.8 times faster on in-cache aggregation, 1.5 times with all hyperthreads) is the argument for fusing the day, the `user_daily` run detection and the counters into the pass that already has the session records in registers, rather than adding passes, where register pressure allows.
- Relaxed fusion's staging point is what the scatter's partition lists already are.

### Gaps
- None of these papers measures hand-written AVX-512 kernels with two hyperthreads of one core both running them; the claim that vector-dense loops lose the most under hyperthreading rests on our own measurement and on Agner's general statement.
- Umbra's and DuckDB's own write-ups on fusion against vectors were not read in this pass.
