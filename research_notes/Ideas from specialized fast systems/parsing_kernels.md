# Parsing kernels: techniques from fast specialized text-to-binary parsers, judged against our fixed-shape CSV line parser

Reading notes. "Ours" means the per-line kernel in `crates/ingest/src/pos/wide/fused/kern.rs` as read on 2026-11-21 (two windows, `tzcnt`/`lzcnt` separators, table rows `ID`/`TS`/`KIND`/`TAIL` keyed by widths, 256-bit shuffle + subtract + multiply-add chains, residues OR-ed, `finish` 8 rows per 512-bit register with compress-in-register and whole stores). Our reference cost, from the brief: about 160 instructions per 77-byte event line at 1.4 to 1.8 instructions per cycle, so about 2.1 instructions per byte and 89 to 114 cycles per line (my division).

Everything under "Cited Findings" is what the source reports. Everything about our parser is under "Inferences" and is my judgment, not measured.

Fetch problems worth knowing: 0x80.pl was read over plain HTTP (its HTTPS certificate is wrong); the VLDB PDFs were converted locally with `pdftotext`; the NoDB paper (two mirrors) and the ClickHouse sources could not be fetched.

## 1. Lowest reported costs for fully converting delimited text to binary, and the loop structures behind them

### Takeaway
No published kernel I found converts a whole multi-field text record to binary for fewer instructions per field than ours already spends: the best single-field kernels cost 33 to 65 instructions each (8-bit integer, IPv4 address, timestamp, 32-bit integer), and whole-record systems that include typed conversion run at 4 to 12 instructions per byte against our 2.1. The published throughput records (21 GB/s Sep, 180 GB/s csveee) are for finding delimiters only; csveee itself measures typed conversion as 4.7 times slower than tokenizing.

### Cited Findings

Whole-record parsers with conversion:

- csveee (Ellmann and Neumann, PVLDB 19(11), 2026; Rust, state of the art as of 2026). Loop: 64-byte vectors, three compares (delimiter, terminator, quote), quote region by carry-less multiply prefix XOR, then three bitmasks per vector: field beginnings B, field ends E, characters to remove R. Fields are assembled from (begin, end) offset pairs and handed to a user closure in the same pass ("fused" parse and process). Single thread 1.8 GB/s quoted mode and 2.1 GB/s unquoted on TPC-H lineitem; peak 181 GB/s unquoted at 198 threads and 167 GB/s quoted at 256 threads on a 128-core AMD EPYC (released Q4 2024), against a measured memory bandwidth of 240 GB/s. — [Ellmann and Neumann 2026](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)
- csveee cost split: the SIMD core is "roughly 1 cycle per input byte" and nearly data-independent; index construction rises to about 0.5 cycles per byte for files with 5 to 10 fields per 64-byte vector; field processing is nearly free unless characters must be removed. — [Ellmann and Neumann 2026](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)
- csveee with conversion: counting records 188 GB/s; parsing one integer column per record 186 GB/s; converting all 16 lineitem columns (integers, floats, dates, chars) 40 GB/s, "4.7x slower". The authors' conclusion: type conversion can dominate, and doing it inline avoids a second materialization pass. — [Ellmann and Neumann 2026](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)
- Same paper, baselines on its hardware: single-threaded counting of lineitem rows is DuckDB 0.6, Rust `csv` 0.7, Umbra 1.2, csveee 2.1 GB/s (Figure 1a); csveee's peak is "22x faster than DuckDB" (DuckDB 1.4.4). — [Ellmann and Neumann 2026](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)
- Instant Loading (Muehlbauer et al., PVLDB 2013; older, SSE 4.2 era). Loop: `pcmpistri` in EQUAL ANY mode finds any of the special characters 16 bytes at a time and branches only when one is found; RANGES mode validates characters; each chunk task parses, deserializes, picks the partition and stores the tuple into a partition buffer that already has the storage layout, in one pass. Parsing plus deserialization of TPC-H text: over 1.6 GB/s with 8 threads on a quad-core machine, against about 1.0 GB/s without SSE; branch misses fall from 194 to 89 per KB of CSV. Baseline profile before the work: 50% of cycles parsing, 20% deserializing, 10% inserting, 20% indexing. — [Muehlbauer et al. 2013](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol6/p1702-muehlbauer.pdf)
- Instant Loading's integer deserializer uses SSE only for strings of 4 or more characters; below that the SSE version was slower than scalar, so it is a hybrid. — [Muehlbauer et al. 2013](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol6/p1702-muehlbauer.pdf)
- simdjson (Langdale and Lemire, 2019; Skylake and Cannon Lake, AVX2). Stage 1 builds an index of structural characters; stage 2 walks it and converts. Fitted cost in cycles, with F floats, S structural characters, B bytes: Skylake stage 1 = 1.7 S + 0.62 B, stage 2 = 19 F + 8.7 S + 0.31 B, total = 19 F + 11 S + 0.92 B. Throughput 2.2 to 3.2 GB/s on record-like files and about 1 GB/s on number-heavy files; 8.3 instructions per byte on average. — [Langdale and Lemire 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.08318)
- simdjson On-Demand (Keiser and Lemire, 2023/2024; one core of a Xeon Gold 6338, Ice Lake, not compiled for AVX-512). Stage 1 index only; values are converted when the program asks, in the type it asks for; unneeded values are skipped by a depth counter and not fully validated. Geometric mean 3.3 to 3.6 GiB/s against 1.9 to 2.1 for the DOM API; 1.3 to 4.4 instructions per input byte on record-like tasks (11.4 on a number-heavy file) against 3.3 to 5.8 (19.9) for DOM. — [Keiser and Lemire 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.17149)
- simdzone (Koekkoek and Lemire, 2024/2025): DNS zone text to wire format, a record-oriented text format with typed fields. Two stages interleaved over a window of up to about 1 MB; AVX2 only. Over 0.8 GB/s single-threaded, 11 to 12 instructions per byte at 3.4 to 3.5 instructions per cycle on a Xeon Gold 6338, 3 to 4 times Knot DNS and about 10 times the old NSD parser. — [Koekkoek and Lemire 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.12035)
- Sep (nietras, .NET; 2025): delimiter finding only at the low level. 21.4 GB/s and 27.3 ns per row on a Ryzen 9950X single thread (0.10.0, 50,000 rows in 29 MB). Loop: two loads, pack 16-bit chars to bytes, four byte compares (`\n`, `\r`, `"`, separator), OR, movemask, skip the block if zero, otherwise handle set bits in turn. Its "floats" benchmark, the nearest to full conversion, runs at 1.26 GB/s and 647 ns per row single-threaded on the same CPU (the post does not say whether that row count includes numeric conversion of every field). — [nietras, Sep 0.10.0](https://nietras.com/2025/05/09/sep-0-10-0/)
- simdcsv (Langdale): the README describes a planned AVX2 delimiter finder with simdjson's carry-less-multiply quote mask; unescaping, numeric conversion and column selection are listed as not done, and it reports no numbers. — [simdcsv README](https://github.com/geofflangdale/simdcsv)
- DuckDB's CSV reader, from a 2024 talk summary (secondary source): a state machine with six states (standard, delimiter, quote, record separator, escape, unquote) driven by character classes; values are cast while reading where possible; threads start mid-file and use the sniffed schema to check that a line is valid. — [notes on Holanda's talk](https://tinfoil-knight.github.io/notes/efficient-csv-parsing---on-the-complexity-of-simple-things-2024)
- One Billion Row Challenge, step-by-step write-up: with unsafe access, SWAR separator search and the branchless temperature parse the row costs 120 instructions and 0.657 branch misses; making the name-length path branch-aware brings it to 98 instructions and 0.084 misses (a row is a name, a short decimal and a hash-table update). — [QuestDB, 1BRC step by step](https://questdb.com/blog/billion-row-challenge-step-by-step/)

Single-field kernels (all assume the field's position is already known):

- Timestamp, 14 digits `YYYYMMDDHHMMSS`, SSE (Lemire 2023, Ice Lake server, GCC 12): load 16 bytes; subtract `'0'`; saturating-subtract a per-digit limit vector `(9,9,9,9,1,9,3,9,2,9,5,9,5,9,-1,-1)`; `pmaddubsw` with `(10,1,...)`; saturating-subtract a per-field 16-bit limit `(99,99,12,31,23,59,59,-1)`; OR the two residues and test for zero; date to epoch in scalar code. 65 instructions and 7.9 per stamp against 700 and 46 for `strptime` (the table gives no unit for the second number). — [Lemire 2023, timestamps](https://lemire.me/blog/2023/07/01/parsing-time-stamps-faster-with-simd-instructions/)
- 32-bit integer of known extent, AVX-512 (Lemire 2023, Ice Lake, GCC 12): 57 instructions and 17 cycles per number, 1.8 GB/s (2.3 GB/s inlined in a loop), against 128 instructions and 39 cycles for `std::from_chars`. Mechanics in section 2. — [Lemire 2023, AVX-512 integers](https://lemire.me/blog/2023/09/22/parsing-integers-quickly-with-avx-512/)
- IPv4 address, four variable-width fields in 16 bytes, SSE (Lemire 2023, Ice Lake): 52 instructions per address with LLVM 16 (63 with GCC 11), 200 million addresses per second, 2.7 GB/s, against 300 instructions for `inet_pton`. — [Lemire 2023, IP addresses](https://lemire.me/blog/2023/06/08/parsing-ip-addresses-crazily-fast/)
- The same problem by Mula (2023): best variants 26.2 cycles per address on a Core i7-6700 (Skylake) and 16.7 to 17.0 cycles on a Xeon Platinum 8375C (Ice Lake), 2.5 to 3 times the best scalar code. — [Mula 2023, IPv4](http://0x80.pl/notesen/2023-04-09-faster-parse-ipv4.html)
- 8-bit integer of 1 to 3 digits, SWAR (Lemire 2023, Ice Lake): about 33 to 36 instructions and 7 to 8 cycles per number, 425 million numbers per second whether lengths are random or sequential; the naive loop does 210 million on random lengths and 365 million on sequential ones. — [Lemire 2023, 8-bit integers](https://lemire.me/blog/2023/11/28/parsing-8-bit-integers-quickly/)
- 16 digits, SSE: subtract `'0'`, `pmaddubsw (10,1)`, `pmaddwd (100,1)`, `packusdw`, `pmaddwd (10000,1)`, then one scalar multiply by 10^8 and add: 0.75 ns on a Ryzen 3900X. — [Kholdstare 2020](https://kholdstare.github.io/technical/2020/05/26/faster-integer-parsing.html)
- Protobuf, for scale of a table-driven binary parser: upb's tail-call parser "over 2GB/s" (2021); hyperpb (2025, Go, Zen 4) gives no absolute figures in text beyond repeated fixed32 at about 20 Gbps. — [Haberman 2021](https://blog.reverberate.org/2021/04/21/musttail-efficient-interpreters.html); [mcyoung 2025](https://mcyoung.xyz/2025/07/16/hyperpb/)

### Inferences
- Per field, ours is already under every published figure: 160 instructions cover two base-36 IDs with unscrambling, two timestamps in three forms, an enum, the two tail fields' shape, packing and the scatter, which is about 23 instructions per field. The published single-field kernels are 33 to 65 instructions each with the field boundaries given for free.
- Read as cycles: simdjson's model would charge a 77-byte line with 8 structural characters about 11 x 8 + 0.92 x 77 = 159 cycles before any number conversion; csveee's core plus index is about 1.5 cycles per byte, or about 115 cycles for our line, again before conversion. Ours is 89 to 114 cycles including conversion and the scatter. csveee's 40 GB/s with full conversion on 128 cores is about 0.3 GB/s per core (my division; the thread count of that row is not stated).
- So the gap to the competing entry's 142 instructions is unlikely to be closed by importing a published kernel. What the literature offers are small substitutions (section 2) and a structural point all the fast whole-record systems share and we already follow: one pass, convert where the line is read, store once in the final layout.
- Sep's result that hand-tuned AVX-512 beats AVX2 by only about 5% at delimiter finding agrees with our dropped "AVX-512 for the masks alone".

### Gaps
- ClickHouse: I found no primary description of its CSV field conversion (only an old header on order-preserving chunked parallel parsing). Not covered.
- No source reports instructions or cycles per record for a CSV record with full typed conversion on Cascade Lake or any AVX-512 CPU; csveee's "complex parsing" row is the only whole-record conversion number and it is a many-core throughput.
- Sep's float benchmark: the column count per row and whether conversion is in the timed region were not in the post.

## 2. Techniques that cut instructions for variable-width fields

### Takeaway
The published toolbox is the one we already use (mask, table row keyed by shape, one shuffle to right-align, subtract, saturating range checks as residues, multiply-add chain). The differences that are new to us are few and small: a masked load that right-aligns without a shuffle table, a hashed whole-shape key with a verify step, quote stripping done on the bitmasks before positions are extracted, SWAR for the rare scalar fields, and (Cascade Lake only, not Skylake-SP) the VNNI fused multiply-adds. I estimate each at 0 to 5 instructions per line.

### Cited Findings

Right-aligning digits:

- Masked load from the field's end (Lemire, AVX-512 BW + VL, so it runs on Cascade Lake): `mask = 0xFFFFFFFF << (32 - digit_count)`; `_mm256_maskz_loadu_epi8(mask, end - 32)` reads the 32 bytes that end at the field's end and zeroes everything before the first digit, so the digits arrive right-aligned with no shuffle and no table. Then masked subtract of `'0'`, masked unsigned compare greater than 9 for validation, `pmaddubsw (10,1)`, narrow 16 to 8 bits, `pmaddubsw (100,1)`, `pmaddwd (10000,1)`, and scalar combination of four 32-bit parts. — [Lemire 2023, AVX-512 integers](https://lemire.me/blog/2023/09/22/parsing-integers-quickly-with-avx-512/)
- One shuffle that both right-aligns and zero-fills (Mula 2018): the control byte 0x80 writes a zero, so a shuffle row built per span pattern yields zero-padded fixed-width numbers ready for the multiply-add chain; example row `[80,1,2,3, 80,80,80,6, 80,9,10,11, 80,80,14,15]` for four numbers of 3, 1, 3 and 2 digits. This is our `IdRow.ctrl`. — [Mula 2018, integer sequences](http://0x80.pl/notesen/2018-04-19-simd-parsing-int-sequences.html)
- Scalar shift alignment (Lemire, Mai): load 4 or 8 bytes, XOR `0x30303030`, shift left by `(4 - len) * 8` to drop the bytes past the number, then one multiply by `0x640a01` and `>> 24` combines hundreds, tens and units; validation is `((x | (x + 0x06060606)) & 0xF0F0F0F0) == 0`. About 20 instructions with no data-dependent branch. The 1BRC temperature parse is the same idea with the decimal point located by `tzcnt(~word & 0x10101000)` and a multiply by `100 * 0x1000000 + 10 * 0x10000 + 1`. — [Lemire 2023, 8-bit integers](https://lemire.me/blog/2023/11/28/parsing-8-bit-integers-quickly/); [QuestDB, 1BRC step by step](https://questdb.com/blog/billion-row-challenge-step-by-step/)
- Eight digits in a 64-bit word, three multiplies: `((x & 0x0f000f000f000f00) >> 8) + (x & 0x000f000f000f000f) * 10`, then the same with masks `0x00ff000000ff0000` / `0x000000ff000000ff` and 100, then `0x0000ffff00000000` / `0x000000000000ffff` and 10000; the masks remove `'0'` so no subtraction is needed. The eight-digit validity test used by simdjson is `((v & 0xF0F0F0F0F0F0F0F0) | (((v + 0x0606060606060606) & 0xF0F0F0F0F0F0F0F0) >> 4)) == 0x3333333333333333`. — [Kholdstare 2020](https://kholdstare.github.io/technical/2020/05/26/faster-integer-parsing.html); [Langdale and Lemire 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.08318)

Keying a table by shape:

- By the raw mask (Mula 2018, SSE): the 16-bit digit/non-digit mask of a 16-byte block indexes a table of 65,536 records, each with a 16-byte shuffle, a selector of the conversion routine (1, 2, 4 or 8 digits per element), the count of elements to store and the bytes consumed. 2 to 5 times the scalar baseline. — [Mula 2018, integer sequences](http://0x80.pl/notesen/2018-04-19-simd-parsing-int-sequences.html)
- By a hash of the mask with a verify step (Mula 2023, IPv4): there are 81 valid dot masks. Variant v7 computes `hash = (dotmask >> 5) ^ (dotmask & 0x03ff)` (maximum 1228), looks up a small id table, then a table of 82 rows, and rejects unless `dotmask == row.expected_dotmask`; the row holds the shuffle pattern and the longest field (1, 2 or 3 digits), which selects one of three conversion kernels. Variant v5 uses a multiply-based hash with maximum 137 and one table. Variant v4 uses nested switches on length then mask. Skylake: v7 26.2, v5 28.1, v4 31.9 cycles. Ice Lake: v4 16.7, v7 17.0, v5 19.4 cycles. So the hashed table won on Skylake and tied with specialized branches on Ice Lake. — [Mula 2023, IPv4](http://0x80.pl/notesen/2023-04-09-faster-parse-ipv4.html)
- Lemire's IPv4 parser: the same 81 masks through a perfect hash; "81 times sixteen bytes" of shuffle rows and about 255 bytes for the hash mapping. — [Lemire 2023, IP addresses](https://lemire.me/blog/2023/06/08/parsing-ip-addresses-crazily-fast/)
- Two-level table (simdutf, 2021): a 12-bit end-of-character mask indexes 1,024 two-byte entries (bytes consumed, shuffle index); the index selects one of 209 16-byte shuffles. The shuffle indices are sorted so that a range compare picks the kernel: 0 to 63 is six characters of 1 to 2 bytes, 64 to 144 four characters of 1 to 3 bytes, 145 to 208 two characters of 1 to 4 bytes. — [Lemire and Mula 2021, simdutf transcoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.10433)
- By a control byte kept apart from the data (Stream VByte, 2017): one control byte holds four 2-bit lengths; it indexes a 256-entry table of 16-byte shuffles (4 KB) and a 256-entry length table; per step: load 16 data bytes, `pshufb`, store 16 bytes, advance the data pointer by the looked-up length. Because control bytes are a separate stream, the next step's table loads do not wait for this step's decode. Up to 4.0 billion integers per second on a 3.4 GHz Haswell, against 2.6 for Masked VByte, which has to derive its key from the data with `pmovmskb`. — [Lemire, Kurz and Rupp 2017, Stream VByte](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.08990)
- Masked VByte itself: 2 to 4 times a scalar VByte decoder (2015). — [Plaisance, Kurz and Lemire 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.07387)

Mixed widths in one shuffle:

- Mula's IPv4 kernel for fields of 1 to 3 digits: one `pshufb` spreads the address into four groups of four bytes: the units digit of each field, the tens digit or zero, the hundreds digit or zero, and a copy of each field's most significant digit (for the leading-zero check); multiply-adds then give four 16-bit values, one compare against 255 gives the range residue, and `packuswb` gives the four bytes. — [Mula 2023, IPv4](http://0x80.pl/notesen/2023-04-09-faster-parse-ipv4.html)

Validating and converting in one step:

- Lemire's two-level saturating checks (per-digit limits before the first multiply-add, per-field limits after it, residues OR-ed, one test) are described in section 1; this is our `lim` and `limv`. — [Lemire 2023, timestamps](https://lemire.me/blog/2023/07/01/parsing-time-stamps-faster-with-simd-instructions/)
- Base64 decoding (Mula 2016): a `pshufb` on a nibble of each byte returns the amount to add to turn ASCII into the 6-bit value, with zero meaning "invalid", so translation and validation share one lookup. — [Mula 2016, base64](http://0x80.pl/notesen/2016-01-17-sse-base64-decoding.html)
- Vectorized classification (simdjson): two `vpshufb` lookups, one by low nibble and one by high nibble, AND-ed, classify every byte into character sets without compares. — [Langdale and Lemire 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.08318)

Optional quotes without a branch:

- csveee moves the quote handling into the bitmasks, before any position is extracted. With C, N, Q the comma, newline and quote masks: `M = prefix_xor(Q)`; `D = (C | N) & !M`; field beginnings `B = D << 1`, then `QB = B & Q` and `B = (B ^ QB) | (QB << 1)`, so a field that opens with a quote begins one byte later; field ends `E = (D ^ (QE << 1)) | QE` with QE the closing quotes, so it ends one byte earlier. A quoted and an unquoted field then yield the same kind of (begin, end) pair and nothing needs removing in the common case. — [Ellmann and Neumann 2026](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)
- Sep takes the opposite route: a compare of the separator mask against the special-character mask branches to a separate path when a quote is present. — [nietras, Sep 0.10.0](https://nietras.com/2025/05/09/sep-0-10-0/)

`pext` and friends:

- csveee checks the field count of every record in a vector at once: `P = pext(R, D)` with D all delimiters and R the record terminators gives one bit per field, set where the field ends a record; P is XOR-ed with a precomputed rotation of the expected pattern (chosen by a `popcnt`), masked to `(1 << popcnt(D)) - 1`; non-zero means a record with the wrong number of fields. The paper notes `pext` is one micro-op with 3 cycles of latency on Zen 5. — [Ellmann and Neumann 2026](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)
- Turning mask bits into positions: simdjson writes eight positions per iteration unconditionally (`tzcnt`, clear lowest bit, repeat) and advances the output by `popcnt`, to avoid a mispredicted loop exit; csveee reports that for CSV, at about 7 set bits per 64-byte vector, "a simple scalar extraction loop performs better" than that unrolled form. — [Langdale and Lemire 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.08318); [Ellmann and Neumann 2026](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)

Keywords:

- simdzone recognizes 67 record-type names by loading the first 8 bytes, upper-casing, masking to the length and hashing with `(value * 3523264710) >> 32` into a 256-entry table, then comparing with the entry. — [Koekkoek and Lemire 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.12035)
- Lemire's small-set test: load 8 padded bytes as an integer, index a 128-byte table with `((x >> 28) ^ (x >> 14)) & 0x78`, compare 8 bytes: 1.1 ns per string on Ice Lake against 2.6 ns for chained 64-bit compares and 7 ns for gperf. A commenter's multiply-high variant uses a 64-byte table. — [Lemire 2022, small set](https://lemire.me/blog/2022/12/30/quickly-checking-that-a-string-belongs-to-a-small-set/)

Instruction-set availability:

- Cascade Lake has AVX-512 F, CD, VL, DQ, BW and VNNI; Skylake-SP has the first five only. IFMA and VBMI start with Cannon Lake; VBMI2, BITALG, GFNI and VPOPCNTDQ on Xeons start with Ice Lake; Zen 4 has all of them. — [Wikipedia, AVX-512](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AVX-512)
- `VPDPBUSD` (VNNI) multiplies four unsigned bytes by four signed bytes, sums the four products and adds the sum to a 32-bit accumulator; `VPDPWSSD` does the same for two pairs of signed words. — [VPDPBUSD reference](https://www.felixcloutier.com/x86/vpdpbusd); [Wikipedia, AVX-512](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AVX-512)

### Inferences

Already ours, with what differs:

- Shuffle row keyed by widths, subtract, saturating limit residues, multiply-add chain: same as Mula and Lemire. Differences: (a) Lemire's kernels use constants held in registers and no per-shape rows; our `TsRow` supplies about 13 operands per line from a 512-byte row (control, base, three weight vectors, two limit vectors, two 64-bit multipliers, date mask and three scalars). The instruction count is the same because loads fold into the instructions, but each is an extra load micro-op. (b) Mula and Lemire verify a hashed key against an expected mask; we index by exact widths and need no verify.
- Both timestamps in one register: I found no published kernel that does this; Lemire's handles one stamp per 128-bit register and converts the date in scalar code (65 instructions for one stamp). Nothing to import.
- The 3-value enum: our `KIND[byte]` row plus one masked compare is already cheaper than either published hash (no hash computation). Nothing to import.
- Base 36: I found no published SIMD base-36 kernel in the sources read (I did not search for one specifically). The nearest, base64's nibble lookup, would replace our single `min(x, x - 39)` with a shift, a mask and a shuffle, so it is worse. Our chain (`pmaddubsw (36,1)`, `pmaddwd (1296,1)`, `pmuludq 36^4`, shift, add) is the minimal form of the decimal chain everyone uses.
- Compress in a register and store whole, advance by `popcnt` (our `finish`): the same idea as simdjson's unconditional index writes.

Candidates not in our parser, with my estimate of the saving per event line:

1. Hashed whole-shape key with verify (Mula v7, Lemire IPv4, simdutf's two-level table). Replace the three `tzcnt`, two clear-lowest-bit and the two row-index computations (`c0 * 64 + c1`, `(c2 - c1) * 2 + q`) by one hash of the first window's comma mask restricted to its first three commas, one small row holding `c0`, `c2`, the `ID` row index and the `TS` row index, and one XOR of the mask against the row's expected mask folded into the residue. Needs the mask cut after the third comma, which itself costs the bit-clearing it is meant to save unless `pdep(0b111, commas)` isolates the three bits in one instruction (BMI2). Estimate: 0 to 4 instructions saved, one more dependent load. Mula's own numbers show the hashed table and the branchy version within 2% on Ice Lake. Low value.
2. Quote stripping on the masks (csveee) so that a quoted and an unquoted field share a table row. It adds 3 or 4 mask operations per window and removes no instruction; it only shrinks the set of hot rows (quoting is 20% per field, so about 4 hot `ID` rows become 1 and about 12 hot `TS` rows become 3). Worth it only if a profile shows L1 misses on the rows, which our "bound by work" finding argues against.
3. Masked right-aligned load for single right-aligned digit fields (the click line's campaign digits, a purchase's price). It removes a table row and a shuffle but needs the mask built from the length (2 instructions) and a masked load. No net saving on the event line; possibly 1 or 2 on a click line. Our ID shuffle cannot be replaced by it because the shuffle also moves the letter, the first digit and the quotes into check positions.
4. SWAR for the purchase tail (3% of rows): `purchase()` re-finds the commas with a 32-byte compare and then calls `price_cents` and `pack_at` per row. The shift-align, validate and multiply form costs about 20 instructions per short decimal field in Lemire's measurement. If `price_cents` plus the product pack are well above about 50 instructions today, this is worth up to about (cost - 50) x 0.03 instructions per line, so 1 to 3. The comma positions are also already known from the per-line `z5`, `z4` and could be carried in the row's class word instead of recomputed.
5. VNNI. `target-cpu=cascadelake` should enable it (check with `rustc --print cfg -C target-cpu=cascadelake`; LLVM may already be emitting `vpdpwssd` for multiply-add followed by add). `vpdpbusd` weights are signed bytes, so at most 127: it can fuse three decimal digits `(100,10,1)` or sums such as `(60,6,1)`, but not `600 h + 60 h + 10 m + m` or the base-36 second level (1296). I found no published digit kernel that uses it. I see at most 1 or 2 instructions per line (folding an add into an accumulator), and it would make the binary fault on a Skylake-SP scorer. Not recommended unless the scorer's CPU is known to be Cascade Lake or later.
6. Constant weights shared by the clock forms and the 13-digit form. The first level `(10,1)` is the same for both (all fields are two-digit decimal pairs if the 13 digits are laid on pair boundaries), so `w1` could be a register constant; `w2`, `w3` and the multipliers differ (60 against 100). This saves load micro-ops, not instructions. Unmeasurable without trying.

What would help but is not on Cascade Lake: a cross-lane byte permute (`vpermb`/`vpermi2b`, VBMI) would gather all four 16-byte field pieces from the line's two windows with one or two instructions in place of our three unaligned loads, one insert and two shuffles; `vpcompressb` (VBMI2) and `vpmultishiftqb` (VBMI) likewise. All need Ice Lake or Zen 4.

### Gaps
- Lemire's IPv4 post does not show the hash or the row layout (only the table sizes); the code is in his repository, which I did not read.
- No source gives a cost for handling optional quotes separately from the rest of the parse.
- Whether LLVM enables `avx512vnni` for `cascadelake` is from my memory of the target definition, not from a source read today.
- VPMULLQ's micro-op count on Intel and an IFMA replacement for our 40-bit unscramble multiplies: not researched (not a parsing question).

## 3. Processing several short records per SIMD step

### Takeaway
I found no published CPU kernel that converts several variable-shape text records per SIMD step and reports a per-record cost. The published record-parallel schemes all rest on a precondition we do not have for the raw text (fixed-width records, elements of equal digit count, or a GPU); the one place they do apply, same-layout data after a canonicalizing shuffle, is where our `finish` already works 8 rows at a time.

### Cited Findings
- Same-class elements in one register (Mula 2018): after the per-pattern shuffle, a 16-byte vector holds sixteen 1-digit, eight 2-digit, four 4-digit or two 8-digit numbers, and one multiply-add chain converts them together; the table picks, per span pattern, the class that converts the most numbers. Reported 2 to 5 times scalar; the speed-up drops when numbers are separated by a variable count of characters, because the number of distinct span patterns rises from about 1,000 to about 28,000, which the author ties to more cache misses in the table and more branch mispredictions. — [Mula 2018, integer sequences](http://0x80.pl/notesen/2018-04-19-simd-parsing-int-sequences.html)
- His "scalar hybrid": build the span mask with SIMD, then run, per byte of the mask, one of 256 pre-compiled scalar routines with compile-time-constant positions; "can be even 2 times faster than the baseline", with very long compile time. — [Mula 2018, integer sequences](http://0x80.pl/notesen/2018-04-19-simd-parsing-int-sequences.html)
- Fixed-width records (a 2025 blog post on HITRAN line lists, AVX2): eight 161-byte records per step, laid out so that the same byte offset of each record falls in the same vector; classification and trimming are vectorized, numeric conversion is left scalar because, in the author's words, fully vectorizing it "isn't worth it" for short fields. No benchmark numbers are given. — [OpenAstronomy 2025](https://openastronomy.org/Universe_OA/posts/2025/08/20250824_1636_prtm2110/)
- Fixed element counts per step in decoders: Stream VByte always produces four integers per shuffle; simdutf produces six, four or two characters per shuffle depending on the table's case. — [Stream VByte](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.08990); [simdutf transcoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.10433)
- GPUs: ParPaRaw (PVLDB 2020) simulates the parsing state machine for all bytes in parallel and reports up to 14.2 GB/s and 4.8 GB in 0.44 s end to end. In csveee's 2026 comparison a GPU parser (CUDAFastCSV, which lacks unescaping and UTF-8 validation) beats csveee for files between 1 MB and 1 GB but is held to about 25 GB/s by the PCIe bus for larger host-resident files. — [Stehle and Jacobsen 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.13415); [Ellmann and Neumann 2026](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)
- Lemire's AVX-512 integer kernel gains 30% (1.8 to 2.3 GB/s) merely from being inlined into a loop over consecutive numbers; it is still one number per step. — [Lemire 2023, AVX-512 integers](https://lemire.me/blog/2023/09/22/parsing-integers-quickly-with-avx-512/)

### Inferences
- Our history runs the other way and agrees with the literature: moving conversion from the 8-row decode (variable shapes per lane, 84 instructions a row) to per-line tables (shuffle to a canonical layout) was the gain. Record-parallel work pays only once rows have the same layout.
- One untested variant follows from Mula's "same class" rule: after the per-line shuffle and subtract, the timestamp bytes are in canonical positions, so the multiply-add chain (about 12 of our instructions per line) could run on two lines per 512-bit register in `finish`. The weights differ only between the clock forms and the 13-digit form, which a mask blend per weight vector, or batching rows by form, would handle. My estimate is 3 to 5 instructions per line saved, against one more 32-byte store per line and more 512-bit multiplies (the heavier clock level on Skylake and Cascade Lake). We measured a related change ("a row's raw bytes copied as vectors and taken apart 8 rows at a time") as no gain, so I rate this low.
- Mula's hybrid (pre-compiled routine per shape reached by an indirect jump) is the published alternative to tables. With 20% independent quoting over 7 fields and three `ts` forms, the shape of a line has about 6 bits of entropy (my arithmetic: 7 x 0.72 + 1.3), so an indirect branch on shape would mispredict on most lines. Tables are the right choice for this data.

### Gaps
- The classic database source on processing a different tuple per SIMD lane (Polychroniou, Raghavan and Ross, SIGMOD 2015, "vertical vectorization") was not fetched by me; it is about operators over binary columns and relies on gathers, which we measured and dropped.
- No source measures a record-parallel text kernel on AVX-512 without VBMI.

## 4. Store counts, table sizes against L1, and two hyperthreads on one core

### Takeaway
The literature is nearly silent on stores per record and on hyperthreads. What it does say: keep the tables that are touched within L1 (the count of distinct rows touched matters, not the table's extent), fewer instructions with a large table can lose to more instructions with a small one, and the two reports on SMT disagree (vectorized parsing gained more from it in 2013; a memory-bound parser gained nothing in 2026).

### Cited Findings
- simdutf: "Tables that fail to fit in the fastest CPU cache (L1) lead to high-latency loads and poor performance"; its own tables total about 11 KiB, against about 105 KiB for Inoue et al. and about 2 MiB for utf8lut. On Zen 2 (Arabic text) utf8lut needs 3.1 instructions per byte at 2.0 instructions per cycle, simdutf 4.2 at 3.1. — [Lemire and Mula 2021, simdutf transcoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.10433)
- Mula's 65,536-entry table: performance falls as the input touches more distinct rows (about 1,000 against about 28,000 patterns), attributed to cache misses and branch mispredictions. — [Mula 2018, integer sequences](http://0x80.pl/notesen/2018-04-19-simd-parsing-int-sequences.html)
- Small tables in the fastest kernels: Stream VByte 4 KB of shuffles plus 256 lengths; Lemire's IPv4 81 x 16 bytes plus about 255 bytes; simdzone a 256-entry keyword table. — [Stream VByte](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.08990); [Lemire 2023, IP addresses](https://lemire.me/blog/2023/06/08/parsing-ip-addresses-crazily-fast/); [Koekkoek and Lemire 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.12035)
- A caution on micro-benchmarks: in Lemire's 8-bit integer post a commenter's 8 MB lookup table is the fastest variant on several compilers, in a benchmark that keeps it hot; in his small-set post a commenter reports that the branchy variants' timings triple when the test set grows from 8,192 to 65,536 entries, which the commenter attributes to branch-predictor capacity. — [Lemire 2023, 8-bit integers](https://lemire.me/blog/2023/11/28/parsing-8-bit-integers-quickly/); [Lemire 2022, small set](https://lemire.me/blog/2022/12/30/quickly-checking-that-a-string-belongs-to-a-small-set/)
- hyperpb argues from the instruction cache: one shared interpreter driven by per-type data tables instead of generated code per type, and one indirect call per field over about 200 handler kinds, on the claim that an indirect branch with a few dozen hot targets predicts well. No cache measurements are given. — [mcyoung 2025](https://mcyoung.xyz/2025/07/16/hyperpb/)
- upb keeps the parser's whole state in six function arguments, which are registers under the calling convention, and reaches every field handler by tail call so that nothing is spilled; fast paths and fallbacks are separate functions so that the fallback does not degrade the fast path's code. — [Haberman 2021](https://blog.reverberate.org/2021/04/21/musttail-efficient-interpreters.html)
- Stores, where stated: simdjson's index extraction always writes eight 32-bit positions per iteration; Stream VByte writes one 16-byte vector per four integers; Instant Loading writes each tuple once, into a partition buffer that has "the same physical layout as the relation partitions"; csveee writes three offset arrays per chunk and then builds slices. None reports a store count per record. — [Langdale and Lemire 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.08318); [Stream VByte](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.08990); [Muehlbauer et al. 2013](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol6/p1702-muehlbauer.pdf); [Ellmann and Neumann 2026](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)
- Hyperthreads: Instant Loading (2013, quad-core) reports that the SSE variant benefits more from Hyper-Threading than the scalar one, reaching 1.6 against 1.0 GB/s at 8 threads, which the authors explain by its use of the vector units beside the scalar ones and its fewer branch misses. csveee (2026, 128 cores) reports that beyond the physical cores SMT gives "diminishing or even slightly negative returns", which it explains as the bottleneck moving to memory bandwidth. — [Muehlbauer et al. 2013](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol6/p1702-muehlbauer.pdf); [Ellmann and Neumann 2026](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)
- Clock: simdzone's table of counters on a Xeon Gold 6338 lists an average frequency of 2.8 GHz for simdzone (AVX2 kernels) against 3.1 to 3.2 GHz for Knot DNS and 3.49 GHz for the scalar NSD parser, single-threaded. The extract I read does not give the paper's explanation. — [Koekkoek and Lemire 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.12035)
- I/O under many threads: `read` into a thread-local buffer reached 183 GB/s on csveee's server against 34 GB/s for one `mmap` shared across threads, which the authors put down to page-table set-up and TLB shootdowns. — [Ellmann and Neumann 2026](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)

### Inferences
- Our tables are large in extent (`ID` 4,161 x 64 B = 266 KB, `TS` 130 x 512 B = 67 KB) but the rows a run touches are few: with 93% of IDs at 9 bytes and 20% quoting, roughly 4 `ID` rows and 12 `TS` rows carry almost all lines, about 6.5 KB. That is inside L1 and consistent with Mula's finding that the distinct rows touched are what matters. The static rows are also shared by both hyperthreads of a core, unlike the per-worker batch and staging buffers. I see no table-size problem; a denser index would not save instructions.
- The simdutf pair (3.1 instructions per byte at 2.0 per cycle against 4.2 at 3.1) is 1.55 against 1.35 cycles per byte by my arithmetic: published evidence for our own rule of judging by cycles under load as well as by instruction count.
- On SMT the two reports are not in conflict with our measurement (the fused parse takes twice as long with two workers per core): Instant Loading's kernel was branchy scalar code with some SSE, which leaves idle resources for a sibling; a dense vector kernel does not. No source measures a vector-heavy parse kernel with both siblings busy.
- csveee's `read` against `mmap` result supports our `pread` choice.

### Gaps
- No source reports L1 miss rates, store-buffer behaviour or SMT scaling for a table-driven SIMD parse kernel. The questions "how many stores per record" and "what happens with two hyperthreads" are effectively unanswered in the literature I could reach.
- No source addresses the 512-bit clock levels of Skylake or Cascade Lake for parsing kernels.

## 5. Skipping work on rarely needed fields (lazy conversion, positional maps, raw filters, speculation on positions)

### Takeaway
All four published forms of "do less" pay under conditions our event lines do not meet: most records discarded (Sparser), few fields wanted from wide records with stable structure (Mison, On-Demand), or the same file read again (NoDB and RAW positional maps). The one that fits, converting a field only for the rows that need it, we already apply to price and product.

### Cited Findings
- Sparser (PVLDB 2018): before parsing, run "raw filters" over the bytes, SIMD searches for a 2-, 4- or 8-byte substring of the predicate's constant, which give false positives but no false negatives; an optimizer samples records, measures each filter's pass rate and cost and the parser's cost, and picks a cascade. Searching a 5 KB record in L1 for one byte value costs over 100 times fewer cycles than parsing it. Up to 22 times faster parsing on queries of low selectivity (Censys), up to 9 times end to end in Spark; 40% of the profiled Spark queries select under 20% of records. It does not support range predicates on numbers. — [Palkar et al. 2018](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p1576-palkar.pdf)
- Mison (PVLDB 2017, Xeon E5-1620 v3, AVX2): builds colon and level bitmaps for a record, then speculates that each wanted field sits at the same ordinal position as in a pattern learned from the first 1,000 records (a "pattern tree", most frequent pattern first); the guess is verified by comparing the field name at the predicted position, and a miss falls back to scanning. 5.5 to 9.1 times the best competitor on Twitter queries of 1 or 2 fields; against Jackson, 3.6 times without speculation and 10.2 times with it, the index-building time being the same in both. Sparser's authors measured Mison at over 2 GB/s per core. — [Li et al. 2017](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol10/p1118-li.pdf); [Palkar et al. 2018](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p1576-palkar.pdf)
- On-Demand (see section 1): converting only requested values in the requested type uses about 60% of the DOM API's instructions (geometric mean 3.1 against 5.1 per byte); the largest gain, 1.3 against 3.3 instructions per byte, is on the task that needs one record out of the file. — [Keiser and Lemire 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.17149)
- Positional maps (NoDB, as described in the RAW paper): on the first read of a CSV file, store the byte position of chosen attributes for every row, for example every 7th or 10th column; a later query jumps to the nearest stored position and tokenizes from there. They help only on repeated access, and they "carry a significant overhead" where positions can be computed from the schema. — [Karpathiotakis et al. 2014](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol7/p1119-karpathiotakis.pdf)
- Column shreds (RAW, PVLDB 2014): generate the scan code for the file and query, and push the read and conversion of a field above the filter, so that it is converted only for rows that pass; an additional 6 times speed-up for highly selective queries over CSV, on the observation that conversion and column building reach up to 80% of query time. — [Karpathiotakis et al. 2014](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol7/p1119-karpathiotakis.pdf)
- csveee's measurement of the same effect: one integer column converted per record costs nothing measurable (186 against 188 GB/s); all 16 columns cost a factor of 4.7. — [Ellmann and Neumann 2026](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)
- hyperpb's analogue for protobuf: fields are tried in an order weighted by a recorded profile, each parser entry linking to the field expected next on a match and to another on a miss, with a hash lookup only after several misses; strings and packed scalars stay as offset and length into the input (not converted). — [mcyoung 2025](https://mcyoung.xyz/2025/07/16/hyperpb/)

### Inferences
- Raw filtering does not apply: every event line is needed, and the only selective predicate, "is a purchase" (3%), already gates the only conversion it could gate.
- Speculation on field positions (Mison, hyperpb) is what our width-keyed tables do without a branch. A predicted-shape fast path would be right at most about a quarter of the time (no quote in the first four fields and the most common `ts` form: 0.8^4 x 0.57 = 23%, my arithmetic), far below the stability Mison relies on.
- A positional map is a second-read optimization; we read once. Its one-pass counterpart, carrying positions forward instead of re-finding them, applies in a small way: `purchase()` recomputes the two tail commas that the per-line code already found.
- Lazy `ingest_ts`: the field is used to pick the copy with the smallest value among duplicates, so in principle only its order matters, and a fixed-form UTC timestamp orders the same as its text. But its validity must still be checked on every line, the date still has to be matched against the memo for that, and its digits ride in the second lane of the register that converts `ts`. Deferring the conversion would save only the ingest half of the date step (about 5 or 6 instructions) while forcing a later read of cold text for the 8% of lines that are duplicates. Not worth it.
- The general condition, from RAW and csveee together: deferring a conversion pays when the conversion is a large share of the per-record cost and a small share of records need it. Price and product met it; nothing else on an event line does.

### Gaps
- The NoDB paper itself (Alagiannis et al., SIGMOD 2012) could not be fetched (HTTP 403 and 404 on two mirrors); positional maps are described here from the RAW paper's summary of it, and NoDB's own numbers are missing.
- Mison's absolute GB/s figures are in charts that the text extraction did not recover; only the ratios above are reported.
