# Move fewer bytes, then shave instructions

Our 0.84 s trails the leaders' 0.31–0.36 s because we do 2.5–3× more total work, not because our loops are slow. The brief's fit (score ≈ 0.05 s + one-core CPU ÷ 3.6) maps our 2.83 core-seconds to 0.84 s and the leaders' scores to just 0.94–1.12, while every hyperthreading, AVX-512 and compiler lever combined is worth perhaps 10–25%. The extra work is data movement the rules never required: every event scattered twice, 24.6M random probes into a 64 MB link table, 1.75 GB of kernel-zeroed fresh memory, and a millisecond-exact sort where 30-minute precision suffices. Attack it in five waves. First, spend one scored run on a calibration probe and build a 4-core × 2-thread Intel test bed, because our dev box's 96 MB L3, license-free AVX-512 and missing performance counters hide exactly the costs that dominate on a Xeon. Second, take two cheap exact wins: coarse-key sessionization, measured at about half the cost of today's sort plus walk, and huge-page insurance. Third, rebuild the middle of the pipeline around one compact scatter, exact dedup inside L2-sized event-ID partitions with a bitmap that clears about 85% of rows without a table probe, person resolution once per distinct ID, and recycled arenas, together worth roughly 0.5–0.85 core-seconds. Fourth, cut parse instructions per row, an estimated 20–35% of its 1.44 s; fifth, add PGO, per-CPU binaries and Ice Lake-gated 512-bit kernels once the structure is stable. If the mostly unmeasured, overlapping estimates hold, the program lands between about 0.3 and 0.55 s, most likely near 0.4 s, so catching the leaders requires most items to land at the top of their ranges. Several famous techniques, among them global Bloom-filter dedup, coroutine interleaving, SMT helper threads, learned sorts and the 1BRC fork-to-skip-munmap trick, do not fit this workload or this sandbox.

## The leaders skip about 1.8 core-seconds of data movement

The brief's two-point fit, score ≈ 0.05 s + one-core CPU seconds ÷ 3.6, reproduces our 0.84 s from **2.83 core-seconds** of work (parse 1.44, dedup and scatter 0.61, sessions 0.70, output 0.08) and puts the leaders at **0.94–1.12 core-seconds**, so every 0.1 core-second removed is worth about 28 ms of score. The divisor fits four physical cores at roughly 1.2× hyperthreading yield, each about three-quarters as fast as our 5 GHz Zen 4 core, which supports reading the scorer's eight vCPUs as 4 cores × 2 threads. The leaders also settle a question that [x86.md](../x86.md) left open: its alternative line through dev-box wall times, scorer ≈ 0.4–0.6 s + 0.4–0.8 × internal, has a floor above the leaders' entire score, so fixed costs such as Docker start-up must be small on the scorer, and one-core CPU is the number to steer by. Serial wall time is the one cost the fit cannot see. The **35–39 ms** the kernel spends unmapping the input after `exit_group` runs on one thread while seven idle, so each of those milliseconds costs a full millisecond of score rather than 1/3.6 of one ([x86.md](../x86.md)).

The x86 profile shows where the excess sits ([x86.md](../x86.md)). The inlined parse takes **51.8%** of samples, and its 1,024-way scatter alone costs about a third of it: eight workers parse 11.3 GB/s into 2 partitions against 7.2 GB/s into 1,024. In the dedup stage, **24.8%** of samples sit on storing a 16-byte `PersonEvent` into a second set of 1,024 buckets, and 9.8% stall behind link-table prefetches with the line fill buffers full; `sort_unstable` takes 6.1% of all samples and kernel page zeroing 6.9%. Casey Muratori calls waste "the biggest multiplier that makes code run slowly" ([Computer, Enhance!](https://www.computerenhance.com/p/waste)), and our waste is bytes moved, not arithmetic. The brief's account of the leader removes exactly these costs. Each event moves once as a 12-byte record keyed by raw user ID; dedup works on 16-byte side records with a small bitmap that proves most IDs unique; the person lookup runs once per distinct user ID; and per-person sorting touches only 4-byte times, with sorting networks for small persons and 30-minute bins for heavy ones.

A bandwidth budget shows why bytes matter more on the scorer than on our box. One Skylake-SP core gets "only 12 GB/s" ([AnandTech](https://www.anandtech.com/show/11544/intel-skylake-ep-vs-amd-epyc-7000-cpu-battle-of-the-decade/12)) against about 80 ns of DRAM latency on Cascade Lake ([Velten et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.03290)). Four cores therefore see at most about 40–48 GB/s, and one thread sustains one random cache-line miss per roughly 7.4 ns ([Groot Koerkamp, PtrHash](https://curiouscoding.nl/posts/ptrhash/)). A 0.3 s run can afford about 3–4 GB of DRAM traffic in total, which leaves at most about 100 bytes of intermediate traffic per event beside the 1.9 GB input, and about 0.25 random DRAM misses per event. The research team's estimates put today's design far outside that budget:

| Design | Intermediate DRAM traffic per event (estimates) |
| --- | --- |
| Today: 32-byte parse scatter, 16-byte person scatter, fresh pages, one link probe per row | about 240 B, plus about 25M random misses |
| One 12-byte scatter, 16-byte by-ID side records, 18.8% of rows moved again | about 97 B |
| Floor with 12-byte records | about 36–40 B |
| Floor with 8-byte records | about 28 B |

Our dev box hides exactly these costs. Its Ryzen 7 7800X3D has a **96 MB L3** that holds the whole 64 MB link table, so link lookups stall for only about 47 ms of CPU there ([x86.md](../x86.md)), while a Skylake-class Xeon offers about 1.4 MB of L3 per core ([7-cpu](https://www.7-cpu.com/cpu/Skylake_X.html)) and sends those probes to DRAM. Zen 4 also runs AVX-512 without Intel's frequency licenses, WSL2 exposes no performance counters, and WSL2's idle reclaim zeroes fresh pages at half speed ([alternatives.md](../alternatives.md)). Deleting DRAM-bound work will therefore pay more on the scorer than our benchmarks show, and wide vectors may pay less or lose. The One Billion Row Challenge playbook, meanwhile, is mostly harvested. The engine already has mmap, SIMD and SWAR parsing, IDs packed into a `u64`, one multiplicative hash per key, prefetched probes, write-combined scatters, 1 MB work-stealing chunks and fast exit, which together cover the largest steps in the published write-ups ([Groot Koerkamp](https://curiouscoding.nl/posts/1brc/); [Topolnik](https://questdb.com/blog/billion-row-challenge-step-by-step/)). What is left is structural.

## One compact scatter replaces two scatters and 22 million probes

The 0.61 core-seconds of dedup, person scatter and link lookups is where our design and the leaders' differ most, and the research converges on one shape for it. Every event is written once as a compact main record into a user-keyed bucket, plus a 16-byte side record into an event-ID partition; dedup runs exactly inside each partition; and persons are resolved once per distinct ID inside each bucket.

### Exact dedup belongs in L2-sized event-ID partitions

Dedup has a hard lower bound. Copies of one event can name different users (fixture E4, about 1 in 64 duplicated events and unbounded in general), so every event ID must meet its copies in some by-ID structure at least once, and no per-user or per-person dedup can be exact ([alternatives.md](../alternatives.md)). The only question is how narrow that structure can be, and a global filter over 20M keys is the wrong answer on four cores. A register-blocked Bloom filter needs about 12–14 bits per key for 1% false positives ([Lang et al., VLDB 2019](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol12/p502-lang.pdf)), about 30–35 MB, which lives in DRAM here. A one-pass "seen before" filter also flags only second and later copies, so finding the 796K first copies takes a second full pass. The two passes move about 4.2 GB at random, against 0.69 GB to write and re-read a 16-byte record per event, and at 7.4 ns per random line they cost at least 0.32 core-seconds before any exact work. Exactness under concurrency would also need atomic test-and-set, because a plain read-modify-write can drop another thread's bit and miss a duplicate; per-thread filters merged afterwards would flag about 43% of singletons. The database literature agrees: a Bloom pre-probe "introduces up to one cache miss per lookup", and radix partitioning wins by up to 3× precisely when tuples are narrow, losing its edge above 32 bytes ([Bandle, Giceva, Neumann, SIGMOD 2021](https://db.in.tum.de/~bandle/papers/bandle-partitionVsNonPartition.pdf)). The global concurrent tables that beat partitioning did so on servers with 45–256 MB of L3 ([Xue & Marcus 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.04153v2)). The planned scheme of sequential side records plus a shared filter therefore needs one change: scatter the side record by event-ID hash, and drop the filter.

The partitioned design is simple and race-free. For every line, valid or not, the parse emits a 16-byte record holding the event ID, `ingest_ts` and a reference to the line's main record; 1,024 partitions hold about 21K records each. An exact open-addressing table of 32K 8-byte slots (256 KB) per partition, or 128 KB at 2,048 partitions, leaves room for both hyperthreads in a 1 MB Cascade Lake L2, whereas today's table is about 1 MB, the whole of it ([alternatives.md](../alternatives.md)). Plain linear probing with a multiply-shift hash is the right scheme at this load: multiply-shift was the fastest hash with every scheme tested, and at 50% load "more sophisticated strategies for handling collisions can not benefit as much" ([Richter, Alvarez, Dittrich, PVLDB 2015](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol9/p96-richter.pdf)). Such tables should not be prefetched; for a 2 MB build side, AMAC's authors measured the no-prefetch baseline 32% faster than group or software-pipelined prefetching ([Kocberber et al., PVLDB 2015](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol9/p252-kocberber.pdf)). Each group's winner is the copy with the smallest `ingest_ts`, chosen before validation (fixture E5), most cheaply as a branch-free minimum over `ingest << 32 | ref`. Losers are killed by setting a drop bit through their reference, and because each main record is referenced by exactly one side record, exactly one task ever writes it, so no atomics are needed.

The leader's bitmap is a refinement inside each partition, not a global structure. Pass one sets a "seen" bit and, on a repeat, a "seen twice" bit, both indexed by spare hash bits; pass two accepts every record whose seen-twice bit is clear and sends the rest to the exact table. That is exact because all copies share an ID and therefore a partition and its bits, the same pre-filter principle behind the 1–2 bytes of hash "salt" DuckDB keeps beside each pointer ([DuckDB](https://duckdb.org/2022/03/07/aggregate-hashtable.html)). Because **2.39M of 21.59M lines (11.1%) sit in multi-copy groups**, no filter can clear more than about 89% of lines; a 128 KB bitmap per partition falsely flags about 2% of singletons and clears about 87%, and a 32 KB one clears about 82%. (One research note's 90–94% used the 4% share of IDs with copies rather than the share of lines.) Three competitive-programming habits sharpen the records further. One odd-constant multiply yields partition, slot and fingerprint at once. Because that multiply is invertible, the 10 partition bits are implied by position and the other 54 bits keep the ID exact. And a 32-bit fingerprint produces only about 53 false pairs across all partitions, which makes an 8-byte fingerprint-plus-reference record viable if every match is verified exactly ([Groot Koerkamp, PtrHash](https://curiouscoding.nl/posts/ptrhash/)).

### Each user ID needs resolving once, not once per row

Today each of about 24.6M event and click rows probes a 64 MB link table that is sized from twice the pair count and rounded up to a power of two, so it runs at 0.29 slot fill ([lib.rs](../crates/links/src/lib.rs)). The loop already prefetches 64 rows ahead and the profile shows full fill buffers, so it sits at its memory-level-parallelism ceiling, and the latency-hiding literature cannot rescue it. Coroutine interleaving executes 5.4× the baseline instruction count in SAP HANA's dictionary lookups ([Psaropoulos et al., PVLDB 2017](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p230-psaropoulos.pdf)), hyperthreading shrinks CoroBase's gain over its baseline from 1.8× to 1.3× ([He, Lu, Wang, PVLDB 2020](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol14/p431-he.pdf)), and once data fits in cache the naive loop beats group prefetching, AMAC and coroutines by up to 2.7–5.1× ([Jonathan et al., PVLDB 2018](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p1702-jonathan.pdf)). The only real lever is fewer probes into smaller tables.

The design with the broadest support across the research, and the closest to the leader's, partitions by raw user ID. The parse scatters a 12-byte main record by the hash of its raw user ID, and the link table is cut into 1,024 slices by the same hash, so each bucket's slice is about 64 KB and the join runs in L2. That is the textbook radix join, where "cache consciousness becomes the decisive factor" ([Balkesen et al., ICDE 2013](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~hebs/course/cs6284/papers/Balkesen-icde2013.pdf)). Each bucket interns its user IDs and resolves each distinct ID once, about **2.0M lookups instead of 24.6M**, all in cache. The one table that maps ID to person and person to local number is a contention-free groupjoin, a pattern that sped some TPC-H queries up to 2× ([Fent & Neumann, PVLDB 2021](https://vldb.org/pvldb/vol14/p2383-fent.pdf)). A small global step homes each multi-bucket person in the bucket of its busiest ID, and only the rows of non-home IDs, **18.8% of event rows**, move again. That removes about 81% of the person-scatter stores and nearly all DRAM lookups, and the 42% of rows whose person has a single event-bearing ID never move twice.

A more radical variant deletes even that 18.8%. Only linked IDs need an exact answer, because an unlinked ID is its own person, so a static retrieval structure over the 1.2M linked IDs suffices: it returns each linked ID's home bucket, and some fixed but meaningless bucket for any other ID. The meaningless answer does no harm, since all rows of an unlinked ID still land together and the bucket-local intern separates IDs by their full value. A PtrHash-style minimal perfect hash costs 2.0–3.0 bits per key and usually one memory access per query ([PtrHash, arXiv 2502.15539](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15539v2)), and a bumped ribbon retrieval structure stores r bits per key with under 1% overhead ([BuRR, arXiv 2109.01892](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01892)). Either "bucket oracle" fits in 1.5–3 MB, 22–43× smaller than today's table, and storing the bucket XORed with a hash of the ID spreads unlinked IDs evenly. If links are resolved before the event parse, the parse's own 1,024-way partition becomes the person bucket and the second scatter disappears. Two cautions apply. Links then sit on the critical path, an estimated 15–30 ms of wall time unless the union-find runs in parallel, for which ConnectIt's UF-Rem-CAS is the consistent winner ([Dhulipala, Hong, Shun, PVLDB 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.03909)). And the ID-resolution note's claim that duplicate rows always share a bucket fails under fixture E4, so the by-ID side channel stays ([alternatives.md](../alternatives.md)). Build the raw-ID design first, and switch to the oracle only if the measured forwarding cost exceeds a links-first phase's.

The union-find needs only a diet. Rem's 1976 algorithm with splicing remains the fastest sequential union-find ([Patwary, Blair, Manne, SEA 2010](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007%2F978-3-642-13193-6_35)); the immediate parent check the engine already uses "significantly improves the performance", and full path compression causes about 7× more load misses than halving or splitting ([Alistarh, Fedorov, Koval, OPODIS 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1911.06347)). Linking under the smaller ID, which the naming rule requires, behaves like randomized linking by index on these random-looking IDs and matches union by rank in expectation ([Goel, Khanna, Larkin, Tarjan](https://users-cs.au.dk/gerth/madalgo/old-madalgo.au.dk/img/SS2013/Course%20material/H-DisjointSetUnionWithRandomizedLinkingMS_Tarjan.pdf)), so rank bookkeeping buys nothing. Sampling methods such as Afforest or k-out gain nothing on a forest with 0.74 edges per node and no giant component; ConnectIt found that "for the sparse case where n = m, k-out sampling and no sampling were fastest" ([ConnectIt](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.03909)). The waste is memory. Numbering the 1.78M endpoints in byte order with a radix sort yields order-preserving ranks, so each node needs a 4-byte parent instead of 16 bytes, the root is automatically the smallest ID that names the person, and the 64 MB table goes away. The randomly touched footprint falls from about 83 MB to about 21 MB, and the 94 ms single-thread build to an estimated 30–50 ms. Link-only IDs must stay, because they can bridge two event-bearing IDs and can be a person's name.

Together, partitioned dedup and once-per-ID resolution should cut the 0.61 core-second stage to roughly 0.15–0.3, **saving about 0.3–0.5 core-seconds**, with the scorer likely at the high end because its link probes go to DRAM. The price is a second scatter stream in the parse, which doubles write-combining state under SMT. Schuhknecht et al. found streaming stores need buffers of fewer than 8 lines, with one line best from 2,048 partitions up, and took 1,024-way partitioning from 2.41 s to 0.96 s with software buffers, streaming stores and fill state kept inside the buffer line ([Schuhknecht, Khanchandani, Dittrich, PVLDB 2015](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol8/p934-schuhknecht.pdf)). Today's 4-line buffers cost 256 KB per worker for one stream ([alternatives.md](../alternatives.md)); about 2 lines per main partition and 1 per side partition keep both streams near 192–256 KB per thread. One suggestion in the notes needs a guard. Flushing each line with a single 512-bit streaming store halves store instructions, but on Skylake-SP or Cascade Lake any 512-bit instruction drops the core, sibling included, into a lower frequency license for about 0.65 ms, so it belongs only in an Ice Lake-or-newer build.

## Sessions need thirty-minute order, not millisecond order

The most valuable structural fact in the rules is that sessionization never needs the order of events within a 30-minute span. Pick a bin width W with W − 1 ms ≤ the gap, for example 2^20 ms or exactly 1,800,000 ms. Any two events of a person in one bin are then at most 30:00.000 apart and share a session, so a break can fall only between consecutive occupied bins, and it falls there exactly when the next bin's minimum minus the previous bin's maximum exceeds 30 minutes. This is the pigeonhole argument behind linear-time maximum gap, which compares "the max of some bucket and the min of the next nonempty bucket" ([Eppstein](https://11011110.github.io/blog/2007/06/18/cute-problem-related.html)), and the grid-cell argument of parallel DBSCAN, whose cells are semisorted rather than sorted because their points "will belong to the same cluster in the end" ([Wang, Gu, Shun, SIGMOD 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.06255)). Stream processing found it too: general stream slicing keeps only partial aggregates plus first and last timestamps per slice, and "session windows ... never require recomputing aggregates" ([Traub et al., EDBT 2019](https://www-live.dfki.de/fileadmin/user_upload/import/10263_traub-efficient-window-aggregation-with-general-stream-slicing-edbt-2019.pdf)). Every output the engine needs from a session, its start, event count, revenue and per-category revenue, is a minimum or a sum, so order inside a bin is irrelevant.

A scratch benchmark written for this research, not yet in the repo, turns the idea into two stable counting passes over a 21-bit key (bin, then person), a branch-free sweep that keeps each group's minimum, maximum and count, and a walk over the groups, which number only about 0.2 per row because events cluster ([coarsebench](file:///C:/Users/Shawn/AppData/Local/Temp/claude/e2243e7e7423ffa4/059307ec-51b9-42e4-965d-09c915346a0e/scratchpad/coarsebench/src/main.rs)). On 20.6M synthetic rows shaped like the generator's data (1,024 buckets; persons with a median of 14 events and a tail up to 400K), single-threaded on Zen 4, it ran at **6.3–7.2 ns/row**, against **8.4–8.9** for an 11-bit LSD radix sort plus walk and **14.1–14.3** for today's `sort_unstable` plus walk. It matched the reference on all 3.2M sessions, on 200,000 adversarial tiny buckets placed on bin edges ±1 ms, and on the hand cases where exactly 30:00.000 joins and 30:00.001 splits. Scaled to the team's harness that is about −5 ns/row, **roughly −0.1 core-seconds**, and more if the in-pipeline sort really costs the 16 ns/row its 45% share of the stage implies. Heavy persons become linear-time: the 400K-event whale costs about 3 ms against roughly 17 ms of work per thread, so its bucket only needs scheduling first. Leis et al. show the price of ignoring such skew: with half the tuples in one partition, parallelism across partitions gave 1.9× and parallelism inside large partitions 5.9× ([Leis et al., PVLDB 2015](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol8/p1058-leis.pdf)). Fusing the sweep and the walk into a single branch-free pass was slower, 7.7–8.1 ns/row, because the select chain became latency-bound.

This differs from the leader's reported mix of sorting networks for small persons and bins for heavy ones. Networks are at least 25% faster than insertion sort on small sets ([Marianczuk, arXiv 1908.08111](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08111)) and might beat two counting passes for the median 14-event person once dense person numbers remove the intern hash, but nobody has measured that here. Coarse keys are measured, exact and uniform across person sizes, so they go first, with a network path for persons of at most 16 events as a later A/B. One robustness caveat applies. With adversarially uniform timestamps every event lands in its own bin, groups per row approach one, and the sweep and walk do more work; the cost stays linear and exact, unlike the repo's hash-based `sess-bins`, which fell back on 964 of 1,024 buckets with uniform timestamps ([alternatives.md](../alternatives.md)).

Three smaller changes ride on the rebuild. If resolution hands the sessions stage a dense person number laid out so that each bucket's persons form a contiguous range, the per-event intern hash and the per-click lookup disappear, an estimated 50–75 ms; mapping rows to dense group indices before vectorized per-group work is the change behind DataFusion's 2–3× grouping speedup ([DataFusion](https://arrow.apache.org/blog/2023/08/05/datafusion_fast_grouping/)). In attribution, the latest-click credit is already O(1) per session, but assists bump a per-day counter once per active campaign per session, 36.9M times. Lazy accounting per person-day records the session index `since[c]` when campaign c enters the window and adds `k − since[c]` when it leaves or the day ends. It is exact, cuts at least a third of the increments at the observed 1.52 sessions per person-day, and removes a variable-trip loop from each of 3.4M sessions, an estimated 15–25 ms. The "last occurrence" trick from competitive programming's distinct-value range queries gives an equivalent form, in which each click covers session starts until the next click of its campaign or the end of its 7-day window, so assists become disjoint range counts ([CSES notes](https://mtuann.github.io/competitive-programming-cpp/practice/ladders/data-structures/offline-tricks/distinctvaluesqueries/)). One bucket-wide LSD pass over clicks, replacing per-person comparator sorts, is worth another 2–8% of the stage. The famous fast sorts solve the wrong problem here: LearnedSort can be up to 45% slower than its predecessor below cache size ([Kristo, Vaidya, Kraska](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2107.03290)), the in-place radix sort IPS2Ra's lead over SkaSort shrinks to small or negative on Intel machines ([Axtmann et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.13569)), and run-adaptive sorts find no runs in shuffled input. The sessions stage should fall by **roughly 0.2–0.3 core-seconds** of its 0.70.

## The parse must shed instructions, because hyperthreads already hide latency

The parse is half of all CPU, and its cost sits in field work rather than in finding delimiters. At about 270 cycles per row on Zen 4, a code-reading count (no PMU under WSL2 can confirm it) gives roughly 400 µops per row: about 100 for the mask tokenizer and flatten, 120–135 for slicing seven fields and stripping quotes, 175 for packing two IDs and parsing two timestamps, and 15 for the scatter push. simdjson's Skylake cost model of 1.7 cycles per structural character plus 0.62 per byte predicts about 72 cycles for an 89-byte row with ten structurals ([Langdale & Lemire](https://arxiv.org/html/1902.08318v7)), so our tokenizer is roughly at the state of the art, and csveee found that converting all 16 TPC-H lineitem columns cut throughput 4.7× relative to counting records ([Ellmann & Neumann, PVLDB 2026](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)). Which tricks pay depends on SMT. Groot Koerkamp's 1BRC entry raised IPC from 2.20 to 2.95 by interleaving four row cursors per thread, but afterwards hyperthreading stopped helping: 0.90 s on 6 threads without it, 0.92 s on 12 with it ([Groot Koerkamp, 1BRC](https://curiouscoding.nl/posts/1brc/)), whereas lehuyduc's C++ entry gained about 62% from hyperthreading ([1BRC discussion #138](https://github.com/gunnarmorling/1brc/discussions/138)). On four cores shared by two threads each, changes that remove instructions beat changes that only overlap latency, so multi-cursor interleaving ranks last here, at 0–5%.

Four changes remove instructions on every CPU. The first flattens commas and newlines into one 16-bit position list and validates a whole block's field counts with csveee's trick: one `pext` of the newline mask under the delimiter mask, compared against a rotation of the expected period-7 pattern ([csveee](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)). Rows then read seven positions at a fixed stride; the newline flatten, block records and per-row popcounts disappear; and the roughly 4.5% of batches holding a malformed row resync through today's exact code, for an estimated 6–10% of the parse. The second validates and strips quotes from mask bits, using masks of quotes at field starts and ends and a `pext` comparison of the two in delimiter order, instead of two byte loads and compares per field, for about 5–10%. It deliberately avoids simdjson's carry-less-multiply prefix XOR, because one malformed line with an odd quote count would flip the parity of every later line in the batch. The third decodes fields in pairs. Both IDs go into one 256-bit register and their 7-bit bytes are packed with `vpmaddubsw` and `vpmaddwd`, the multiply-add packing that let Muła and Lemire's AVX2 base64 decoder run at about 0.25 cycles per byte against 1.8 for Chrome's scalar code ([Muła & Lemire](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1704.00605)), while the ISO-Z `ts` (58% of rows) is validated together with the always-ISO-Z `ingest_ts` against one two-stamp template, for 8–15%. Each piece needs its own measurement, since Lemire's SIMD timestamp parser still spends 65 instructions on a simpler stamp ([Lemire 2023](https://lemire.me/blog/2023/07/01/parsing-time-stamps-faster-with-simd-instructions/)) and is not automatically cheaper than our date-table parser at 1.9–4.2 ns on the M4. The fourth parses timestamp forms in homogeneous batches. Forms drawn at random in a 58/10/32 mix cost at least 0.42 mispredictions per row even with a perfect predictor, a branch-free select lost 10% on the M4, and selection-vector "refill" gave up to 30–34% in compiled query pipelines ([Lang et al., VLDB J 2020](https://15721.courses.cs.cmu.edu/spring2023/papers/08-vectorization/lang-vldbj2020.pdf)); Topolnik's 1BRC entry fell from 2.4 s to 1.8 s on branch-statistics restructuring alone ([Topolnik, QuestDB](https://questdb.com/blog/billion-row-challenge-step-by-step/)). With overlap, these four come to **roughly 20–35% of the parse, about 0.3–0.5 core-seconds**.

Register pressure is a cheap fifth lever on x86's 16 general registers. Materializing seven 16-byte `Field` structs per row forces spills, so fields should be consumed as they are produced, and quoted fields, IDs over 9 bytes and malformed lines belong in `#[cold] #[inline(never)]` functions. Jon Gjengset's 1BRC stream code shows the Rust tools for this, `select_unpredictable` for genuine 50/50 choices, `assert_unchecked` for invariants, `cold_path` hints and unchecked splits, though it publishes no timings ([jonhoo/brrr](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jonhoo/brrr/main/src/main.rs)). Groot Koerkamp found a never-taken loop free only when wrapped in an always-false `if`, and fixing his out-of-bounds `get_unchecked` undefined behaviour cost him 1.44 s → 1.56 s, a reason to prefer asserted invariants to unchecked indexing ([1BRC](https://curiouscoding.nl/posts/1brc/)). csveee also found a plain extraction loop faster than simdjson's unrolled flatten on sparse masks, an A/B worth running because our blocks average about 4.3 commas against 8 unconditional stores.

AVX-512 adds most only on Ice Lake or newer. There, VBMI2's `vpcompressb` decodes bitsets at 0.29 ns per value against 0.74 for the unrolled loop ([Lemire](https://lemire.me/blog/2022/05/10/faster-bitset-decoding-using-intel-avx-512/)), simdjson's 512-bit kernel minifies at 12 GB/s against 4.3 GB/s for its 256-bit kernel ([Lemire](https://lemire.me/blog/2022/05/25/parsing-json-faster-with-intel-avx-512/)), and compress-based de-quoting could delete per-field quote logic, for an estimated 10–30% of the parse. Cascade Lake lacks VBMI2, and there 512-bit code would drop the whole parse phase into a lower frequency license, so the right tool is AVX-512BW/VL at 256 bits, for 2–5%. Never benchmark the memory-destination compress on the Zen 4 box, where it runs about 10× slower than the register form ([Go runtime commit](https://remotebranch.eu/Stowage/go/commit/041f564b3e)). Several tempting shortcuts fail on this data. Only about 10% of rows match the most common byte layout, so shape speculation would mispredict most of the time; Sparser-style raw filtering ([Sparser](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~matei/papers/2018/vldb_sparser.pdf)) has nothing to filter when nearly every row survives; and lazy validation of dedup survivors saves at most the 7% duplicate share at the price of a second pass. Every data-dependent fast path also needs an exact fallback: 1BRC entries tuned to the 413 original station names lost ground on the 10,000-key variant ([Morling](https://www.morling.dev/blog/1brc-results-are-in/)).

## Fresh pages, exit teardown and Intel quirks tax every run

### Recycle memory and insure huge pages before anything clever

By the brief's numbers, kernel zeroing and fault handling take about a fifth of all CPU, roughly 0.57 core-seconds for 1.75 GB of fresh memory per run, and no container setting removes them. Anonymous memory "is initialized to zero", and `MAP_UNINITIALIZED` works only on kernels built with an option "normally enabled only on embedded devices" ([mmap(2)](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/mmap.2.html)). At the 8–11 GB/s per core that Ice Lake reaches clearing 2 MB pages ([Arora patch series](https://patchew.org/linux/20250414034607.762653-1-ankur.a.arora@oracle.com)), each 100 MB of fresh memory costs about 9–12 ms of core time. The fix is to touch less. Give each worker one 2 MB-aligned arena carved into chunks that return to a thread-local free list once a stage has consumed them, so the next stage writes into recycled lines and fresh memory shrinks to the peak live set. With 12-byte records that should bring fresh memory to about 0.4–0.7 GB and **save 0.2–0.35 core-seconds**, more if the scorer is a cloud VM with the idle reclaim that halves zeroing speed on our box. Two corollaries follow. Never return memory with `MADV_DONTNEED` mid-run, since later touches become zero-fill faults again ([madvise(2)](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/madvise.2.html)), and point non-temporal stores at recycled lines, because into fresh pages they pay one DRAM write for the kernel's zeroing and another for ours. Once value ranges are known, person records can also shrink from 16 to 8 bytes with field widths chosen at run time and a 16-byte fallback, which stays exact without hard-coding anything.

Huge pages are cheap insurance against an unknown scorer. With THP off, our baseline ran **18% slower** (558 against 472 ms) ([x86.md](../x86.md)). The engine can read `/sys/kernel/mm/transparent_hugepage/enabled`, align every arena to 2 MB, call `madvise(MADV_HUGEPAGE)` under the `madvise` mode, and fall back to `MADV_COLLAPSE` under `never`, since on Linux 6.1 and later it "ignores these settings" ([kernel THP docs](https://docs.kernel.org/admin-guide/mm/transhuge.html)). Prefaulting with `MADV_POPULATE_WRITE` before collapsing would zero 4 KB pages and then copy them, so the two must not be combined. The gain is zero under `always` and up to about 15% of the score otherwise. A cleverer variant, collapsing the input's page cache into huge pages through an executable mapping, could speed later scored runs but exploits state that survives between runs and depends on the host's kernel configuration; skip it.

The input mapping is the one remaining serial cost. After `exit_group` the kernel spends **35–39 ms** clearing about 470,000 4 KB page-table entries on one thread; unmapping during dedup made dedup slower through TLB shootdowns ([alternatives.md](../alternatives.md)), and the 1BRC fix of letting a child process own the mapping cannot work for PID 1, because the kernel waits for every task in the namespace before init's exit completes ([pid_namespace.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/torvalds/linux/master/kernel/pid_namespace.c)). Reading with `pread` into reused per-worker buffers removes the teardown at the price of copying 1.9 GB, about 0.16–0.19 core-seconds across eight threads, which the fit prices at about 45–55 ms of score against the 35–39 ms it saves; that is close enough, and the copy's side effects on faults and cache uncertain enough, that only an A/B can decide it. csveee measured reads into reused buffers at 183 GB/s against 34 GB/s for partitioned mmap, but on 128 cores, where mapping contention dominates ([csveee](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)). `io_uring` is blocked by Docker's default seccomp profile ([Docker docs](https://docs.docker.com/engine/security/seccomp/)), and `O_DIRECT` would throw away the warm page cache.

Pipeline shape matters less than bytes, but two rules from database engines apply. Fuse compute-bound steps and stage memory-bound ones: compiled, fused pipelines ran 74% faster than vectorized ones on compute-heavy TPC-H Q1, while vectorized ones ran 32% faster on hash-probe-heavy Q9 ([Kersten et al., PVLDB 2018](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p2209-kersten.pdf)). So the parse stays fused with its scatter, and each partition's dedup check, intern, sessionizing, attribution and aggregation run as one pass that writes straight into output column buffers without materializing sessions. And fill barrier tails with independent work, such as link and click parsing, union-find and the next stage's arena setup, from one morsel-style queue ([Leis et al., SIGMOD 2014](https://db.in.tum.de/~leis/papers/morsels.pdf)), which trims wall time though not CPU. The roughly 100 ms of single-threaded allocation that x86.md once measured between stages is already gone with `alloc-parallel` ([x86.md](../x86.md)).

### Treat hyperthreads and AVX-512 as hazards until a probe reports

Every hardware lever's sign depends on facts we lack, so the first step costs a few microseconds of stderr in one scored run: CPU model and flags (`avx512bw`, `avx512vbmi2`), sibling lists, THP mode, kernel release, the `Speculation_Store_Bypass` line of `/proc/self/status` and the cgroup's `cpu.max`. Locally, `docker run --cpuset-cpus` over four sibling pairs emulates 4 cores × 2 threads once a busy-loop test confirms that WSL2's reported siblings really share a core, and an 8-vCPU Intel AVX-512 cloud VM is the decisive test bed. The SMT mechanics favor small per-thread footprints. Intel statically partitions the store buffer between active hyperthreads, and the Fallout authors measured Skylake's 56 entries halving whenever the sibling runs any code, while fill buffers and load ports are shared without partitioning ([Intel](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/software-security-guidance/technical-documentation/intel-analysis-microarchitectural-data-sampling.html); [Fallout](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.12701)). Scatters are therefore the most SMT-sensitive code we have: two scatter workers on one core hold 512 KB of staging today, half a Cascade Lake L2. Hyperthreading added only about 11% on average on a recent Xeon ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/review/intel-xeon-678x-ht)), and 3% on memory-bound TPC-H Q6 against 33% on Q3 ([Kersten et al.](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p2209-kersten.pdf)). Sweep `SS_PART_BITS` from 7 to 10 under the sibling cpuset, compare 4 and 8 threads per stage, and pin workers to sibling pairs, for an estimated 0–10%.

AVX-512's sign flips with the CPU generation. On a Skylake-SP Xeon W-2104, a light 512-bit integer `vpor` dropped the core from 3.2 GHz to 2.8 GHz (−12.5%) and held it there about 650 µs after the last such instruction, while the 256-bit form changed nothing ([Travis Downs](https://travisdowns.github.io/blog/2020/01/17/avxfreq1.html)); Cloudflare saw AVX-512 on 10% of requests cut a server's total throughput by 5.5% ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/on-the-dangers-of-intels-frequency-scaling/)). The license belongs to the physical core, so the sibling almost certainly pays too, though no source measured it. Ice Lake client parts lose at most 100 MHz ([Travis Downs](https://travisdowns.github.io/blog/2020/08/19/icl-avx512-freq.html)), and Sapphire Rapids shows no fixed 512-bit offsets ([Chips and Cheese](https://chipsandcheese.com/p/a-peek-at-sapphire-rapids)). The rule is to select 512-bit kernels at run time only when `avx512vbmi2` reports Ice Lake or newer, use AVX-512VL at 256 bits on Skylake-SP and Cascade Lake, and put the whole hot chunk loop inside one `#[target_feature]` function, which stable Rust supports since 1.89 stabilized the AVX-512 target features and intrinsics ([Rust 1.89](https://blog.rust-lang.org/2025/08/07/Rust-1.89.0/)). Shipping v3, v4 and Ice Lake builds and picking one in `run.sh` with `exec` keeps the binary PID 1 and adds 0–4%; LLVM already prefers 256-bit vectors on Intel AVX-512 targets ([llvm-commits](https://lists.llvm.org/pipermail/llvm-commits/Week-of-Mon-20190909/693481.html)).

PGO comes last because every structural change makes its profile stale. On a Ryzen 5900X it cut roxmltree's run time 7–12% and quick-xml's 25.5% ([roxmltree #118](https://github.com/RazrFalcon/roxmltree/issues/118)), and rustc's wall time 10–16% ([Inside Rust](https://blog.rust-lang.org/inside-rust/2020/11/11/exploring-pgo-for-the-rust-compiler/)), but it gave nothing on our M4, so budget 0–8%, with BOLT at 0–3% on top ([BOLT](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06735)). Three traps await. Cargo takes exactly one rustflags source, so a `RUSTFLAGS=-Cprofile-generate` build silently drops the `[target]` flags, including `target-cpu=x86-64-v3` and the compile-time AVX2 and BMI2 paths ([Cargo reference](https://doc.rust-lang.org/cargo/reference/config.html#buildrustflags)). The profile is written at normal exit, which today's `std::process::exit` honors and a raw `_exit` would not. And dispatched kernels that never run during training become cold. If the probe reports a forced store-bypass mitigation, which kernels before 5.16 applied to every seccomp-filtered process ([kernel seccomp pull](https://lore.kernel.org/lkml/163581426904.14115.15371811525127876559.pr-tracker-bot@kernel.org/T/)) at an Intel-reported cost of 2–8% ([Packt](https://www.packtpub.com/en-lu/learning/tech-news/intels-spectre-variant-4-patch-impacts-cpu-performance)), keep scatter cursors in registers, since the process cannot switch the mitigation off. Two SMT designs stay experimental. Pairing a parse sibling with a scatter sibling on each core echoes Zhou et al.'s cooperative operators, which beat naive parallelism by 26–29% when L2 conflicts dominated, but it could land anywhere from 5% slower to 15% faster on the parse. Helper threads that preload for their sibling beat naive parallelism by only 4.3–10% in the same study ([Zhou et al., VLDB 2005](https://www.vldb.org/conf/2005/papers/p49-zhou.pdf)), and little DRAM-bound work will remain for them after the rebuild.

## Five waves, ordered by certainty per engineering day

Ranking by expected saving times confidence divided by effort, and respecting dependencies, gives five waves. The probe comes first because it costs one submission and fixes the sign of several later items. Coarse-key sessionization and huge-page insurance come next because they are exact, contained and either measured or nearly free. The rebuild is the largest item and the one the leaders validate, but it touches the parse, dedup, links and sessions at once, so it follows the quick wins and uses the probe's cache sizes and topology. Parse instruction cuts follow because the rebuild changes what the parse emits, although the tokenizer and field-decoder work is independent and can run in parallel. Toolchain levers go last because profiles and SMT sweeps go stale with each structural change. This revises the repo's current amd64 list, which puts `rec-16`, `reader-pread` and link-table huge pages ahead of any structural change ([alternatives.md](../alternatives.md)): `rec-16` becomes part of the rebuild, link-table huge pages become moot once the 64 MB table is gone, and `reader-pread` drops to a measured coin flip.

Summed without netting out their overlaps, these estimates remove about 1.05–1.9 core-seconds, leaving 0.9–1.8 and a score of roughly 0.30–0.55 s, most likely near 0.4 s. Only coarse-key sessionization and the THP penalty are measured; everything else is derived from sample shares, published per-access costs and code reading, on a machine that misrepresents the scorer's caches and vector units. The rows also interact: smaller records cut both scatter cost and fresh memory, and once lookups are per distinct ID, latency hiding stops mattering. Treat the range as a ranking aid, not a forecast, and re-rank after the probe.

Every wave needs the same guardrails. Exactness comes first: differential tests against a naive reference on generated data with injected corner cases (cross-user copies across workers and files, an invalid earliest copy, equal-`ingest_ts` ties, a giant duplicate group, gaps of exactly 30:00.000 and 30:00.001 across bin edges and UTC midnight), plus sabotage builds that force 4-bit fingerprints or send every ID to one partition so the exact fallbacks actually run. Groot Koerkamp's 1BRC "no counting" shortcut shifted a station's mean from 22.8 to 22.7 ([1BRC](https://curiouscoding.nl/posts/1brc/)), the kind of failure these tests exist to catch. Speed comes second: one-core per-stage CPU for instruction-level work, the sibling-pinned cpuset for anything touching fan-out, buffers or vector width, and fresh containers after 12 s of idle for final calls, where a leader-level run corresponds to roughly 0.21–0.25 s of internal time on the dev box ([x86.md](../x86.md)). The research also clears away a list of celebrated techniques. Global filter-first dedup and global concurrent hash tables move several times the bytes of a partitioned pass on a four-core slice. AMAC, coroutines and helper threads add memory-level parallelism to loops that will no longer be DRAM-bound. Learned, in-place radix and AVX-512 general-purpose sorts, run-adaptive sorts and sliding-window structures solve orderings the rules never ask for. Afforest, label propagation and Shiloach-Vishkin target giant components this forest lacks, and a global dense renumbering of event user IDs would need the same 24.6M random probes it is meant to avoid. On the platform side, `MAP_HUGETLB` needs a hugetlb pool and privileges ([mmap(2)](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/mmap.2.html)), and allocator swaps already lost to tuned glibc in our own tests.

## Conclusion

The exploitable structure lives in the rules, not in the input's order. The events arrive shuffled, so run-adaptive and partially-sorted-data algorithms have nothing to grip, but the rules hand over three kinds of slack: sessions need order only to 30-minute precision, uniqueness needs proof only for the 11% of lines that share an ID, and person identity needs exactness only for the 1.2M linked IDs. Each slack turns an exact global operation into a coarse, cache-local one plus a small exact fix-up — counting passes on coarse keys, a bitmap in front of a tiny table, a retrieval structure in front of a seeded intern — and that one pattern explains most of the leaders' design. It is also the best source of future ideas, better than any catalogue of micro-tricks: for each stage, ask which bits of the input the output provably does not depend on, and stop computing them.

Measurement errs in a predictable direction. Hyperthreading is already priced into the scorer's 3.6 divisor, so tricks that only raise IPC, such as interleaved cursors, helper threads or coroutines, are partly pre-harvested by the sibling thread, while removing instructions and bytes moves the score directly. The dev box's 96 MB L3 understates what deleting DRAM-bound lookups is worth, and its license-free AVX-512 overstates what wide vectors are worth on a Skylake-derived Xeon. A local null result for lookup removal should not kill it, and a local win for 512-bit kernels should not ship until the probe has named the CPU.
