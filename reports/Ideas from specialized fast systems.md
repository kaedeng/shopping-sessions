# Fast systems leave only single-digit gains

**Nothing in the five research strands is worth more than about 1 to 3% of CPU time to this pipeline, and no technique was found that would by itself close a large gap.** The ideas that make the fastest parsers, contest entries and database kernels fast are already in the tree: conversion where the line is read through shape-keyed tables, one pass and one store per record, a single up-front reservation, `pread` into a cache-sized buffer, a staged streaming scatter, parallel release before exit. No published kernel converts a field in fewer instructions than ours. What is new and usable falls into two groups: four nearly free changes that are insurance for the Intel scorer and cannot be validated on Zen 4 (jump alignment for the Cascade Lake erratum, buffer sizes for a hyperthread pair, a prefetch of the scatter's staging line, pinning), and a few work items after the parse estimated at about 1% each (memory reuse, a smaller click record, sorting networks for mid-sized click lists). Three further items that the notes rank highly are already in the uncommitted working tree. Almost every gain figure below is a researcher's estimate; the source-measured figures behind them (the jump erratum at 0 to 3% on affected Intel cores, 16-byte against 8-byte records in a scatter at 1.31 to 1.69 times, one Intel store quirk, sorting networks at about 8 times `std::sort`) were taken on other hardware and other code. The research is worth more for what it closes (twenty lines of inquiry, listed at the end) and for one warning: the 7800X3D hides several Intel costs, so the most productive hour left before the deadline is a paired run on rented Cascade Lake and Sapphire Rapids instances, not another kernel.

**Row 1 is the best ratio of gain to effort in the research, on one condition.** On Skylake through Cascade Lake a microcode update keeps any jump that crosses or ends on a 32-byte boundary out of the decoded-µop cache. Microsoft measured 0 to 3% on SPEC CPU 2017 with outliers to 10%, and the padding that avoids it costs about 3% of code size ([Microsoft C++ blog](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/cppblog/?p=25462)). The hyperthreads note says LLVM does not pad by default for `cascadelake`, and a block that falls out of the µop cache goes to the legacy decoders, which two hyperthreads use in alternation ([Intel optimization manual](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall13/cos217/reading/ia32opt.pdf)). The gain is zero on Ice Lake and later and invisible on Zen 4, so the only test available here is that nothing gets slower.

**Rows 2 and 3 come from how the scatter sits in a shared Intel core.** `Chunks` stages 192 bytes per list with the fill count in a separate two-byte array, so at the notes' figure of about 1,300 lists a worker's staging is roughly 250 KB. It lives in L2, and every record store is a store to a line that misses L1 and hits L2, among stores that hit L1. On Intel that pattern is measured at 9 or 18 cycles a store, and a prefetch or a load of the line beforehand removes it ([Travis Downs, Intel performance quirks](https://github.com/travisdowns/uarch-bench/wiki/Intel-Performance-Quirks)). The held batch already carries every record's list number before `Chunks::extend` stores anything, so a pass of prefetches is small. The same arithmetic puts 500 KB of staging plus two input chunks against the **1 MB of L2 that a Skylake-SP or Cascade Lake pair shares**, where Intel's rule is to block for a quarter to a half of the cache per logical processor ([Intel optimization manual](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall13/cos217/reading/ia32opt.pdf)) and the partitioning literature finds a single staged line best from 2,048 partitions upward ([Schuhknecht et al.](http://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol8/p934-schuhknecht.pdf)). Our sweeps of chunk size, partition size and staged lines were ties on Zen 4, so the smaller settings cost nothing here. The same paper's in-line fill state (1.12 to 1.39 times there) will give less than the analytics note hopes: the fill array is 2.6 KB and stays in L1, the case in which the paper itself found separate small arrays better.

**Row 5 is the only lever on the kernel's share, and it is smaller than the notes suggest.** Zeroing fresh pages was 7.5% of the cycles in `positional.md`'s profile, taken before the lists became chunk chains, so the share is lower now and needs re-measuring. The kernel zeroes a 2 MB page at about 12 GB/s on one thread ([folio_zero_user series](https://lkml.iu.edu/2601.0/06689.html)). On Ubuntu-configured kernels before 6.13, where `init_on_alloc` is on by default, a huge page was zeroed twice ([kernel patch](https://lkml.rescloud.iu.edu/2410.1/06900.html), [Launchpad 1862822](https://bugs.launchpad.net/bugs/1862822)). No unprivileged call turns either off, so the lever is touching fewer new pages. DPDK's per-core cache of free objects ([DPDK mempool](https://doc.dpdk.org/guides/prog_guide/mempool_lib.html)) and TigerBeetle's shared, statically sized pools ([TigerBeetle](https://tigerbeetle.com/blog/2022-10-12-a-database-without-dynamic-memory/)) give the pattern: the block handed out next is the one freed last, already faulted and still in cache. The operating-system note puts the reachable share at a third to a half of the zeroing. As far as I read the tree, it is nearer 1% of CPU time: the split stages re-read the event lists when a person turns out to have two runs, so those 345 MB cannot be recycled while the fused stages run. What can be reused is the click lists after grouping (about 80 MB at 16 bytes a click) and everything in `parsed` after the fused stages. Today `parsed` is released to the kernel just before the classic lane and the output fault fresh pages, which is the pattern the note warns against: a release followed by a fresh fault pays an unmap, a TLB flush and a second zeroing.

**The kernel items after the parse are small, and two of the notes' estimates are stale.** The 8-byte click record rests on a measured effect, 16-byte entries scattering 1.31 to 1.69 times slower than 8-byte ones depending on the method ([Schuhknecht et al.](http://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol8/p934-schuhknecht.pdf)), and on the rule that an unbuffered scatter should stay inside the private cache. Click grouping is about 4% of cycles, hence the 1 to 1.5%. The sorting idea is worth less than the note's 1 to 2%: `Attr::light` already reads lists of at most 16 clicks unsorted, so only `Attr::heavy`'s `sort_unstable` remains, where networks on whole registers are the fastest published method (about 8 times `std::sort` on small arrays, [Bramas](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.08579); 256 keys in several hundred cycles, [vqsort](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.05982)). The hyperthreads note's advice to replace `vpmullq`, split the decode's multiplies into 256-bit halves and cut its spills was sized against the 8-row decode that commit `afa2de4` replaced. `finish` now has four `vpmullq` per 8 rows, and each needs the low 40 bits of a 40-by-40-bit product, which a single `vpmuludq` cannot give.

## The 7800X3D hides Intel costs that only an Intel run can price

**The scorer is more likely Ice Lake or later than Cascade Lake, and probably not Skylake-SP.** In late 2026 an 8-vCPU Intel instance with AVX-512 is Skylake-SP or Cascade Lake only on older families (AWS C5 and M5, Google N2 and C2, some Azure Dv3 and Dv4 pools) and Ice Lake, Sapphire Rapids, Emerald Rapids or Granite Rapids on everything newer ([AWS general purpose](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/ec2/latest/instancetypes/gp.html), [AWS compute optimized](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/ec2/latest/instancetypes/co.html), [Azure](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/virtual-machines/sizes/general-purpose/d-family), [Google Cloud](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/cpu-platforms)). Two pieces of our own evidence lean the same way. The organizers suggest `target-cpu=cascadelake`, which enables VNNI and so excludes Skylake-SP if they meant it literally (my inference from `.cargo/config.toml`). And the scorer's ratio to our paired time was 1.12 without 512-bit code and 1.15 to 1.19 with it (`positional.md`), a 3 to 6% difference that fits Ice Lake or later, or a Cascade Lake already at its middle clock level, but not a full step down, which is about 15% for light 512-bit code on all cores of a Xeon Gold 5120 ([Lemire](https://lemire.me/blog/2018/09/07/avx-512-when-and-how-to-use-these-new-instructions/)).

The per-thread windows are those sizes halved, and Agner Fog describes the same buffers as competitively shared, so read them as a lower bound.

**The clock penalty is a Cascade Lake risk only, and it has moved into the sessions stage.** On Skylake-SP and Cascade Lake a single 512-bit instruction puts the core, and so both hyperthreads, at the middle licence, and the return comes no sooner than about 650 µs after the last one ([Travis Downs](https://travisdowns.github.io/blog/2020/01/17/avxfreq1.html)). Sustained 256-bit multiplies are in the same class, and the lowest licence needs roughly one heavy 512-bit instruction per cycle ([Lemire](https://lemire.me/blog/2018/09/07/avx-512-when-and-how-to-use-these-new-instructions/)), far denser than `finish`. So the parse probably sits at the middle level whatever its register width, and its 512-bit step should stay; whether five 256-bit multiply-adds a line count as "sustained" is documented nowhere the researchers could find. Cloudflare lost 10% of throughput with 2.5% of CPU time in 512-bit code, because of the hold ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/on-the-dangers-of-intels-frequency-scaling/)). That is the case to watch after the parse: `positional.md` still says nothing else uses a 512-bit register, but `attr-wide` and the new row passes do. Ice Lake-SP keeps three levels with much smaller drops ([Intel, Hot Chips 32](https://www.hc32.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day1/HotChips2020_Server_Processors_Intel_Irma_ICX-CPU-final3.pdf)), and Emerald Rapids measured 2.95 against 3.01 GHz ([Phoronix](https://mail.phoronix.com/review/intel-5th-gen-xeon-avx512/5)).

**The sources confirm our 2.0 times under a sibling, and the notes disagree on its cause.** Agner Fog's rule is that code aiming above two instructions per cycle gains nothing from a second thread in the core ([Agner Fog](https://www.agner.org/optimize/microarchitecture.pdf)). Cloudflare's 512-bit cipher kept about half of one thread's throughput per core with both siblings busy, clock drop included ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/on-the-dangers-of-intels-frequency-scaling/)). Two 1BRC authors lost their whole hyperthreading gain once they interleaved cursors ([curiouscoding.nl](https://curiouscoding.nl/posts/1brc/), [buybackoff](https://hotforknowledge.com/2024/01/13/1brc-in-dotnet-among-fastest-on-linux-my-optimization-journey/)). On Skylake-X the gap between a fused and a vectorized engine shrank when all hyperthreads ran ([Kersten et al.](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p2209-kersten.pdf)). The practical rule is to judge a kernel by the cycles it holds shared ports and shared cache, not by its instruction count. On which resource binds our per-line loop on Intel, the hyperthreads note infers port 5 and proposes two lines per 512-bit register; the parsing note rates the same change at 3 to 5 instructions a line. Counting from the current source, not from disassembly, I find about a dozen port-5 µops and nine port-1-only bit scans and `pext` a line, against a line of roughly 100 cycles (the parsing note's division). That is not a saturated port, and nobody has measured it. uiCA simulates a loop body for Cascade Lake and Ice Lake, usually within 1% of measurement for one thread ([uiCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.14210)); it answers the question in an hour, though it models neither a sibling nor cache misses.

**Three host conditions move our numbers with no action open to us.** Speculative store bypass is disabled in every container on the development box, but a default Docker container gets it forced only when the host kernel is older than 5.16 and runc older than 1.2 ([seccomp for 5.16](https://lkml.iu.edu/hypermail/linux/kernel/2111.0/00943.html), [runc changelog](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/opencontainers/runc/main/CHANGELOG.md)), so store-heavy loops run faster on a current scorer than here, never slower. The page-aligned read buffer avoids a 25-fold `rep movsb` slowdown on Zen 4 ([glibc bug 30994](https://sourceware.org/bugzilla/show_bug.cgi?id=30994)) and matters at most twofold on Intel ([lkml](https://lkml.rescloud.iu.edu/2111.2/04911.html)). And the double zeroing above depends on a kernel build default that cannot be read from inside the container; the operating-system note's check is to time the first 2 MB faults at start, about 170 µs each at 12 GB/s and about twice that when doubled.

**One Intel run decides rows 1 to 4 and the clock question together.** The hyperthreads note names m5n.2xlarge (Cascade Lake) and m7i.2xlarge (Sapphire Rapids) as 8-vCPU examples ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/ec2/latest/instancetypes/gp.html)). The paired harness there prices the jump padding, the staging prefetch, the buffer sizes and pinning in one sitting. Two cautions from contest authors apply to any change in how memory is released. A release that outlives the process lands in the next run: a forking 1BRC entry spread from 181 to 345 ms over 100 runs where a non-forking one held 206.8 ± 3.6 ms ([1BRC discussion #495](https://github.com/gunnarmorling/1brc/discussions/495)), so check that back-to-back runs match spaced ones. And counters per row are steadier than seconds ([Topolnik, QuestDB](https://questdb.com/blog/billion-row-challenge-step-by-step/)), which our instruction counts already follow.

## Conclusion

The research says the pipeline has converged on the designs of the fastest published systems, so the literature no longer holds a multiple. What separates it from the faster entry recorded in `positional.md` is not a named technique but instructions and cycles inside our own kernels, and the lever on that is the profile, not another import. For a score that follows CPU time on hyperthreads the yardstick also changes: µops on the shared ports and bytes of shared cache, neither of which the 7800X3D prices the way a Xeon does.

For the day that is left, that argues for an order. Take the jump padding and the pair-sized buffers if paired runs show no loss, because they cost minutes. Spend an hour on Intel hardware before building anything larger, because it prices four rows of the ranking at once and settles whether Cascade Lake behaviour matters at all. Start the memory reuse only if a per-stage fault count shows more than a few hundred MB first touched after the parse. Everything in the last table can stay closed.
